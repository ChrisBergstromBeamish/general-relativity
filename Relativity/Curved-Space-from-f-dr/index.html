Curved Space from f · dr

The curvature of space, derived step by step from the conservation of mass-energy, set beside Einstein's field equations and merged with them by algebra, with the exact places where the two part.

An article of the section Relativity of Physics: Particle Reconstruction and General Relativity, written for teachers and curious readers. It comes from the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy.

People like to see Einstein's field equations, even when the indices look like a swarm of bees. So here they are, first:

\[ R_{\mu\nu}-\frac12\,R\,g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}. \]

On the left is the curvature of space and time. On the right are the energy and the stresses of whatever is there. General relativity's derivation of them, as the paper writes it out in Section 5, rests on twenty-one assumptions, among them one spacetime with one metric that every observer reads, and a class of equations chosen in advance.

The paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy reaches the curvature from one idea: "the curvature between two points is the integral of f dot dr." This page is that derivation, every step shown, plain words beside each, and worked with numbers. Then it writes Einstein's equations in the same variables, does the algebra that merges the two, and shows exactly where they part.

Do the two say the same thing in different ways? The algebra decides, and its answer has two parts. Around one body at rest, outside its event horizon, they give the same clocks, the same rulers and the same curvature, by different routes. In three places they part, by exact amounts.

The solution at a glance. For the frames held at rest around a body, outside \(L=2G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/c^2\):

\[ f=\sqrt{1-\frac Lr},\qquad d\ell=\frac{dr}{f},\qquad d\ell^2=\frac{dr^2}{1-L/r}+r^2\,d\Omega^2, \]
\[ \text{curvature of space: }\ K_{r\theta}=K_{r\varphi}=-\frac{L}{2r^3},\quad K_{\theta\varphi}=+\frac{L}{r^3};\qquad \text{many bodies, the clocks: }\ f^2=1-\sum_a\frac{L_a}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_a|}. \]

The symbols are explained just below, and the rest of the page shows where each line comes from.

What goes in, and the symbols

The derivation uses only these (Section 4):

  1. The conservation of mass-energy and of momentum (Section 2). Draw a closed surface and let nothing cross it: the complete energy inside, every kind counted once in one observer's units, does not change. The paper calls it the count. Its mass equivalent is \(\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}=E_{\mathrm{enc}}/c^2\).
  2. The paper's premises 1 to 6 (Section 3).
  3. Special relativity at one place, between two frames that pass each other there.
  4. Planck's relation, with one constant in every frame.
  5. Newton's law far from the mass, which fixes \(G\).

Nothing else. In particular: no equation of Einstein's, no one geometry shared by all observers, no general covariance, no condition that the equations be of second order, and no assumption that the arrangement is the same at every time.

Step 1. The idea: f dot dr

The method, in the author's words (Section 4):

  • "Figure out the integral of f dot dr."
  • "Figure out how much energy is gained. Get the mass equivalent and divide it by the mass."
  • "The curvature between the two points will be the same as the ratio in (2) above."

Here is the mathematics of the first two. A body of rest mass \(m_0\) falls freely past frames held at rest. A frame held at rest reads its energy as \(mc^2=\gamma m_0c^2\) (premise 2). Over a step \(d\boldsymbol\ell\) the pull \(\mathbf g\) does work on the body's mass as it is at that moment, energy already gained included:

\[ dW=m\,\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell,\qquad dm=\frac{dW}{c^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dm}{m}=\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2},\qquad \ln\frac{m_2}{m_1}=\int_1^2\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2}. \]

In words: turn the work into mass, divide by the mass, and add up along the way. The pull always acts on the current mass, so the small gains compound like interest, and the sum is a logarithm.

Step 2. The clock

Now the third step. The surface around everything is closed, so the count inside cannot change, and neither can its pull (premise 1). The body's gain must therefore be cancelled where it occurs (premise 3). What cancels it is the clock rate of the frame held at rest there, which falls by the same ratio:

\[ \frac{f_1}{f_2}=\frac{m_2}{m_1}=\exp\!\left(\int_1^2\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2}\right), \qquad d\ln f=\frac{g_{\mathrm{hold}}\,d\ell}{c^2},\qquad \gamma f=e\ \ \text{constant}. \]

In words: the falling body gets heavier by exactly the share by which the local clock runs slower. Counted in A's units, its energy \(e\,m_0c^2\) never changes. This ratio of clock rates is what the paper means by the curvature between two points.

Three readings follow (statement 1):

  • an energy that the held frame reads as \(E\) counts as \(fE\) in A's units, so its unit of energy is \(f\) of A's;
  • a clock counts the ticks of a transition, and with the same Planck's constant the held frame's clock runs at \(f\) of A's;
  • light that climbs from the held frame to A is redshifted by \(1+z=1/f\).

Nothing in these steps asks what makes the pull. They hold between any two points, for any pull.

Step 3. The ruler

How fast is the falling body going as it passes a frame held at rest? The paper answers by equating two integrals of f dot dr (statement 4).

Around the body, for a fall from rest far away, the three steps give

\[ \ln\gamma=\int_\infty^{r}\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2}=\ln\frac1f . \]

In empty space, a force \(\mathbf F\) that sets a body in motion does the same kind of work on its current mass, and special relativity gives the same ratio for the same integral:

\[ \frac{dm}{m}=\frac{\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{mc^2},\qquad \ln\gamma=\int\frac{\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{mc^2}. \]

Set the two equal. The falling frame is in exactly the motion that the same f dot dr would generate there:

\[ \gamma=\frac1f,\qquad v=c\sqrt{1-f^2}. \]

The falling frame needs no force. No work is done on it, so it keeps A's clock, rulers and mass (premise 4). The two frames simply move past each other, and special relativity between them, at that place, changes the clock and the ruler along the motion and leaves the sideways rulers alone. So the frame held at rest has

\[ \text{its clock at } f \text{ of A's},\qquad \text{its ruler along the force at } f \text{ of A's},\qquad \text{its sideways rulers the same as A's}, \]

and a step \(dr\) along the force, on A's ruler, reads on the held frame's own ruler

\[ d\ell=\frac{dr}{f}. \]

In words: picture two people at the same place, one standing on a platform, one falling past. The faller is going exactly as fast as a push in empty space with the same f dot dr would make them go. For two people who move past each other the rule is already known, and it gives the standing person's clock and ruler.

Step 4. The pull through every sphere: the clock rate

The count sets the pull through every sphere around a point mass (premise 1). Counted in A's units the pull is \(f\,g_{\mathrm{hold}}\), so

\[ 4\pi r^2\,f\,g_{\mathrm{hold}}=4\pi G\,\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}} . \]

Put in the clock step and the ruler. Since \(d\ell=dr/f\),

\[ g_{\mathrm{hold}}=c^2\,\frac{d\ln f}{d\ell}=c^2\,\frac{df}{dr},\qquad f\,g_{\mathrm{hold}}=c^2f\,\frac{df}{dr}=\frac{c^2}{2}\,\frac{dF}{dr}, \]

and the flux law becomes

\[ \frac{c^2}{2}\,\frac{dF}{dr}=\frac{G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}}{r^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2\,\frac{dF}{dr}=L \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;F=f^2=1-\frac Lr,\qquad L=\frac{2G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}}{c^2}\;} \]

with the clock rate set to 1 far away, where clocks agree with A's.

In words: counted in A's units, the pull is Newton's law, exactly, at every strength. The factor \(1/f\) in the ruler cancels the factor \(f\) in the counting.

The same result, written as f dot dr itself: between any two places the square of the clock rate changes by twice the integral of the counted force over the path, per unit of the probe's rest energy \(\mu c^2\),

\[ F(r_2)-F(r_1)=\frac{2}{\mu c^2}\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\frac{G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}\,\mu}{r^2}\,dr . \]

Two things come free (statement 6). The falling frame passes the held one at \(v=c\sqrt{1-f^2}=\sqrt{2G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/r}\), Newton's speed of fall. And on its own clock the falling body obeys Newton's law exactly: \((dr/d\tau)^2=c^2(e^2-F)\), so \(d^2r/d\tau^2=-G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/r^2\).

At \(r=L\) the clock rate of a frame held at rest is zero: a zero, not a singularity. Everything up to the section "Where the two part" is for the outside of \(L\). What holds at \(L\) and beyond comes from premises 7 and 8, not from carrying any formula there (statement 6).

Step 5. The space the held frames measure

Along the radius, a held frame measures \(d\ell=dr/f\). Around the body its rulers are A's, so a circle has the circumference \(2\pi r\) for both. The space that the frames held at rest measure is therefore

\[ \boxed{\;d\ell^2=\frac{dr^2}{f^2}+r^2\,d\Omega^2=\frac{dr^2}{1-L/r}+r^2\big(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\big)\;} \]

With the clock added, the readings of the frames held at rest are

\[ ds^2=-F\,c^2dt^2+\frac{dr^2}{F}+r^2\,d\Omega^2 , \]

which is general relativity's solution outside a round body (Section 7), reached here with no field equation. The frames that fall freely from rest far away read the same clocks and rulers as

\[ ds^2=-c^2dT^2+(dr+v\,dT)^2+r^2\,d\Omega^2,\qquad dT=dt+\frac{v}{c^2F}\,dr , \]

with A's time \(T\) and flat space (premise 4). Outside \(L\) the two say the same thing. So "the curvature of space" below means the curvature of the space that the frames held at rest measure. The frames that fall measure flat space, and each reading is real for the frame that makes it (premise 7).

Step 6. The curvature of that space, from the definition

Write a prime for the change per unit of distance on the held ruler, \(d/d\ell\). The ruler gives \(r'=dr/d\ell=f\). Differentiating once more, with \(f^2=1-L/r\),

\[ r''=\frac{df}{d\ell}=f\,\frac{df}{dr}=\frac12\,\frac{dF}{dr}=\frac{L}{2r^2}. \]

The planes that hold the radius. Cut space through the centre, for example along the equator. On that surface lengths are \(d\ell^2+r(\ell)^2\,d\varphi^2\), and Gauss's curvature of such a surface is

\[ K_{r\varphi}=-\frac{r''}{r}=-\frac{L}{2r^3}\qquad(\text{and }K_{r\theta}\text{ the same}). \]

In words: on a flat sheet the circles around a point grow at a steady rate, \(r''=0\). Here they grow faster and faster on the way out, \(r''\gt0\), so the surface curves like a saddle, and its curvature is negative.

The plane of the sphere. A sphere of radius \(r\) has the curvature \(1/r^2\) on its own. The space around it subtracts the square of the rate at which the spheres grow outward, \((r'/r)^2\) (Gauss's equation):

\[ K_{\theta\varphi}=\frac1{r^2}-\Big(\frac{r'}{r}\Big)^2=\frac{1-r'^2}{r^2}=\frac{1-F}{r^2}=+\frac{L}{r^3}. \]

In flat space \(r'=1\) and the two terms cancel. Here \(r'=f\lt1\): the spheres grow more slowly than in flat space, and what is left is \(L/r^3\).

The same, from the definition written in full (Section 10, Part 8, its part in space; the definition of curvature is mathematics). With \(\ell,\theta,\varphi\) as coordinates, so that \(d\ell^2+r^2d\theta^2+r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\), the connection coefficients are

\[ \Gamma^\ell{}_{\theta\theta}=-rr',\quad \Gamma^\ell{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-rr'\sin^2\theta,\quad \Gamma^\theta{}_{\ell\theta}=\Gamma^\varphi{}_{\ell\varphi}=\frac{r'}{r},\quad \Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta,\quad \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi}=\cot\theta , \]

and the curvature \(R^\alpha{}_{\beta\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\nu\beta}-\partial_\nu\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta}+\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\beta}-\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\beta}\) gives

\[ R^\theta{}_{\ell\theta\ell}=-\partial_\ell\Big(\frac{r'}{r}\Big)-\Big(\frac{r'}{r}\Big)^2=-\frac{r''}{r},\qquad R^\theta{}_{\varphi\theta\varphi}=(1-r'^2)\sin^2\theta . \]

Divided by the squared unit lengths, these are \(K_{r\theta}=-r''/r\) and \(K_{\theta\varphi}=(1-r'^2)/r^2\).

The curvature of the clock rate. The same definition, applied to the clock rate, measures how \(f\) bends from place to place on the held rulers: \(C_n=(1/f)\,\partial^2f/\partial\ell_n^2\) along a straight line in the direction \(n\). Along the radius, \(f'=f\,df/dr=L/(2r^2)\) and \(f''=f\,d(f')/dr=-fL/r^3\). A straight line across the radius, a distance \(s\) from where it touches the sphere, has moved outward by \((r'/r)\,s^2/2\) on the held ruler, so along it the clock rate bends by \(f'r'/r\):

\[ C_r=\frac{f''}{f}=-\frac{L}{r^3},\qquad C_\theta=C_\varphi=\frac{f'r'}{fr}=+\frac{L}{2r^3}. \]

These are the parts of the curvature of space and time that hold time (Section 10, Part 8). All four, collected (Section 4, the physicist's box):

\[ \boxed{\;C_r=-\frac{L}{r^3},\quad C_\theta=C_\varphi=+\frac{L}{2r^3},\qquad K_{r\theta}=K_{r\varphi}=-\frac{L}{2r^3},\quad K_{\theta\varphi}=+\frac{L}{r^3}\;} \]

Each is one or two derivatives of the clock rate. One number does not depend on who looks (Sections 10 and 12):

\[ K=R_{abcd}R^{abcd}=4\big(C_r^2+2C_\theta^2+2K_{r\theta}^2+K_{\theta\varphi}^2\big)=\frac{12L^2}{r^6}=\frac{48G^2\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}^2}{c^4r^6}. \]

Integrating the curvature gives back the clock. The curvature between two points (the ratio of the clock rates, from f dot dr) and the curvature at a point (two derivatives of the clock rate) are one statement read two ways (Section 12, statement 3). Write \(N=f\). Integrating \(N''=C_r\,N\) and \(r''=-K_{r\theta}\,r\) twice along the held ruler, from a place A to a place B (two places held at rest, not the far observer A) a distance \(D\) farther out (Section 10, Part 9):

\[ N_B=N_A+D\,N'_A+\int_0^D(D-s)\,C_r\,N\,ds,\qquad r_B=r_A+D\,r'_A-\int_0^D(D-s)\,K_{r\theta}\,r\,ds , \]

with the curvature taken at the radius reached at each step, and at A: \(N_A=f_A\), \(N'_A=L/2r_A^2\), \(r'_A=f_A\). Start at \(r_A=2000\), with \(L=1000\), and go 9000 outward on the held rulers. The radius at B comes out 9990.8 and the clock rate there 0.948635. The integral of f dot dr of step 4, \(F(r_B)=F(r_A)+L\,(1/r_A-1/r_B)\), gives the same 0.948635. A clock at B runs 1.342 times as fast as a clock at A, and a photon sent from A is read at B at 0.745 of its frequency at A.

Step 7. The equations to set beside Einstein's

The same steps, done for any closed surface and not only for a sphere, give the clock around any arrangement of point masses (statement 7). Put point masses \(\mathcal M_a\) at the places \(\mathbf x_a\), at rest in the frame of the calculation, and do the calculation in that one frame, with its ruler, scale and clock. At every place the held frame's ruler along the force is at \(f\) (premise 5), and once more the factor \(1/f\) of the ruler cancels the factor \(f\) of the counting:

\[ f\,\mathbf g_{\mathrm{hold}}=\frac{c^2}{2}\,\nabla F . \]

The pull counted through any closed surface, through the areas of that frame's grid, is set by the count inside it (premise 1). That the pull is counted through the areas across the force as that frame's grid measures them is derived for one point mass (statement 4) and for every round arrangement (Section 17); for other arrangements it is carried over, and the paper's premise table names it (statement 7). This is the equation of the derivation that stands where Einstein's field equations stand:

\[ \boxed{\;\oint f\,\mathbf g_{\mathrm{hold}}\cdot d\mathbf A=\frac{c^2}{2}\oint\nabla F\cdot d\mathbf A=4\pi G\,\mathcal M_{\mathrm{inside}}\;} \]

At a point of empty space it reads

\[ \boxed{\;\nabla^2F=0\;} \]

and with \(F\to1\) far away its solution is

\[ \boxed{\;F(\mathbf x)=f^2=1-\sum_a\frac{L_a}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_a|},\qquad L_a=\frac{2G\mathcal M_a}{c^2}\;} \]

In words: on the left is geometry, the pull counted through a surface, which is the slope of the squared clock rate summed over that surface (or, by the divergence theorem, the curvature of the clock rate summed over the volume inside); on the right is the count inside. It has the shape of Einstein's equations, curvature on one side and energy on the other. And it is linear in the counts, so the deficits \(1-f^2\) of the point masses add, exactly.

When the bodies move, the rulers are updated instantaneously (premise 6), and the same sum holds at every moment \(T\), with each body's place and count at that moment (statement 8):

\[ F(\mathbf x,T)=1-\sum_a\frac{L_a(T)}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_a(T)|},\qquad \frac{d\mathbf P}{dT}=\sum_i\mathbf F_i(T)=0 . \]

The pulls on two bodies are equal and opposite at every moment, so the total momentum never changes.

The curvature form. Around one point mass, the curvatures of step 6, which come from the clock of step 4 and the ruler of step 3, obey three sets of relations. In empty space:

\[ \boxed{\;C_1+C_2+C_3=0,\qquad K_{na}+K_{nb}-C_n=0\ \ \text{for each direction } n,\qquad K_{12}+K_{13}+K_{23}=0\;} \]

Here \(a\) and \(b\) are the two directions at right angles to \(n\). The first is the flux law written at a point: the three curvatures of the clock rate add up to what the count there makes them, and empty space holds no count. For the second, take \(n\) along the radius: \(-L/2r^3-L/2r^3=-L/r^3=C_r\); take \(n\) across it: \(-L/2r^3+L/r^3=+L/2r^3=C_\theta\). The third is the sum of the three curvatures of space. These are the equations to set beside Einstein's.

Einstein's field equations (the comparison side)

This section is general relativity's own derivation, as the paper gives it in Section 5. It is the comparison side: nothing from the f dot dr derivation is used in it. Among its twenty-one assumptions:

  • one spacetime with one metric, and every observer measures components of this same metric (A1);
  • the equivalence principle (A2), and ideal clocks and rulers that read the metric (A3);
  • equations of the same form in every coordinate system (A4);
  • matter entering through one stress-energy tensor \(T_{\mu\nu}\), conserved locally (A7, A8);
  • a geometric side built from the metric alone, of second order, with a divergence that vanishes identically (A9), and a source side that is one constant times \(T_{\mu\nu}\) (A10);
  • Newton's law far from the mass (A11), and a cosmological constant of zero (A12).

From these, with the constant fixed by Newton's law,

\[ R_{\mu\nu}-\frac12\,R\,g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu},\qquad R_{\mu\nu}=0\ \ \text{in empty space}. \]

For an arrangement at rest, write the metric with the clock rate \(f\) and the rulers \(h_{ij}\) of the frames at rest, \(ds^2=-f^2c^2dt^2+h_{ij}\,dx^idx^j\). With \(\varepsilon\) the energy density that the frame at rest reads, \(S_{ij}\) the stresses and \(S\) the sum of the three pressures, the equations become (Section 5, part 10)

\[ \begin{aligned} (35)\qquad&\Delta_hf=\frac{4\pi G}{c^4}\,f\,(\varepsilon+S),\\ (36)\qquad&R_{ij}(h)-\frac{D_iD_jf}{f}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\Big[S_{ij}+\tfrac12\,h_{ij}\,(\varepsilon-S)\Big],\\ (37)\qquad&R(h)=\frac{16\pi G}{c^4}\,\varepsilon . \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\Delta_hf=D_iD^if\) is the Laplacian of the clock rate on the rulers \(h_{ij}\), \(D_iD_jf\) are its second derivatives along those rulers, and \(R_{ij}(h)\) is the Ricci curvature of the space those rulers measure, with \(R(h)\) its sum over the three directions. The first is the time-time part, \(R_{00}=f\,\Delta_hf\). The second holds the parts in space. The third is the time-time part of the Einstein tensor, \(G_{00}/f^2=R(h)/2\).

To first order, for an arrangement that changes (A19), the equations become a wave equation for \(\bar q_{\mu\nu}\), the first-order change of the metric, trace-reversed, with the speed \(c\) (part 13):

\[ \Box\,\bar q_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{16\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu},\qquad \Box=\nabla^2-\frac1{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}. \]

The algebra that merges the two

Einstein's still equations in the curvatures of step 6. Along a unit direction \(n\), \(D_nD_nf/f\) is \(C_n\). In three dimensions \(R_{nn}(h)\) is the sum of the curvatures of the two planes that hold \(n\), \(K_{na}+K_{nb}\), and \(R(h)=2(K_{12}+K_{13}+K_{23})\). So (35) to (37) read (Section 12)

\[ \begin{aligned} (35)\qquad&C_1+C_2+C_3=\frac{4\pi G}{c^4}\,(\varepsilon+S),\\ (36)\qquad&K_{na}+K_{nb}-C_n=\frac{4\pi G}{c^4}\,(\varepsilon-S+2P_n)\quad\text{for each direction } n,\\ (37)\qquad&K_{12}+K_{13}+K_{23}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,\varepsilon , \end{aligned} \]

with \(P_n\) the pressure along \(n\). In empty space every right side is zero, and these are the three boxed equations at the end of step 7, one for one. The curvatures that f dot dr gives satisfy every one of them. So around one point mass the clocks and rulers of the f dot dr derivation satisfy Einstein's equations for empty space, \(R_{\mu\nu}=0\) (Section 4, "In Einstein's terms"). Here that is a result, not a starting point.

One round body, line by line. Einstein's route to the clocks and rulers of one body (Section 7) starts with two unknowns: the clock \(N(r)\) and the ruler \(B(r)\), with \(d\ell=B\,dr\). In those variables, with a subscript \(r\) for \(d/dr\) and \(r'=1/B\), the four parts of step 6 are

\[ C_r=\frac{N''}{N},\qquad C_\theta=\frac{N_r}{N\,r\,B^2},\qquad K_{r\theta}=\frac{B_r}{r\,B^3},\qquad K_{\theta\varphi}=\frac{1-1/B^2}{r^2}. \]

Now take Einstein's equations one at a time and see which f dot dr result each one becomes.

Line 1: (35) plus (36) along the radius. The \(C_r\) cancels, and what is left is

\[ 2\,(C_\theta+K_{r\theta})=\frac{2}{rB^2}\,\frac{d\ln(NB)}{dr}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,(\varepsilon+p_r) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d\ln(NB)}{dr}=\frac{4\pi G\,rB^2}{c^4}\,(\varepsilon+p_r), \]

equation (46) of Section 7, with \(p_r\) the pressure along the radius. In empty space \(NB\) is constant, and 1 far away:

\[ NB=1,\qquad d\ell=\frac{dr}{N}. \]

This line becomes step 3, the ruler \(d\ell=dr/f\). Einstein gets it by adding two field equations (Section 7, part 7). The f dot dr derivation gets it by equating the two integrals of f dot dr, with no field equation (statement 4; Section 9).

Line 2: (35) alone. In general,

\[ C_r+2C_\theta=\frac{1}{N\,r^2B}\,\frac{d}{dr}\Big(\frac{r^2N_r}{B}\Big)=\frac{4\pi G}{c^4}\,(\varepsilon+S). \]

In empty space \(r^2N_r/B\) is the same on every sphere, and since \(4\pi r^2\,N g_{\mathrm{hold}}=4\pi r^2c^2N_r/B\) (Section 7, part 18), that says the pull counted through every sphere is the same. With \(NB=1\) it reads \(r^2\,dF/dr=\text{constant}\). This line becomes step 4, the flux law. The f dot dr derivation also gives its value, set by the complete count inside, \(r^2\,dF/dr=L\), at every strength. Integrated over a region, (35) sets the pull counted through the surface around it, \(\oint f\,\mathbf g_{\mathrm{hold}}\cdot d\mathbf A\), equal to \(4\pi G\) times a mass that general relativity calls the Komar mass (Section 5, part 12). In the f dot dr derivation that mass is the complete count, and this equation is where the derivation starts (premise 1; Section 6; Section 7, part 19).

Line 3: (36) across the radius. With \(NB=1\), in empty space,

\[ K_{\theta r}+K_{\theta\varphi}-C_\theta=\frac{1}{r^2}\Big(1-\frac{d(rF)}{dr}\Big)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d(rF)}{dr}=1,\qquad F=1+\frac{C}{r} \]

(Section 7, part 8). Einstein's route then fixes \(C=-2GM/c^2\) by Newton's law far away, one constant \(M\) for every frame (part 9; Section 17). In the f dot dr derivation this line is already met. Step 4 gave \(F=\text{constant}-L/r\), and \(d(rF)/dr=1\) is the statement that the clocks agree with A's far away, \(F\to1\).

Line 4: (37). With \(NB=1\) the sum of the three curvatures of space is the left side of line 3, so outside the matter (37) adds nothing. Inside matter it sets general relativity's ruler. In a round space \(R(h)=(2/r^2)\,d[r(1-1/B^2)]/dr\), so, with the ruler finite at the centre,

\[ \frac{1}{B^2}=1-\frac{\ell(r)}{r},\qquad \ell(r)=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\int_0^r\varepsilon\,u^2\,du , \]

the ruler that the paper calls general relativity's interior ruler, \(ds/d\ell=\sqrt{1-\ell(s)/s}\) (Section 17). Outside the matter \(\ell=L\), and it is the ruler of step 3. Inside, the two derivations part (the second parting below).

So, for one body at rest, outside \(L\): Einstein's still equations for empty space come down to three statements, \(NB=1\), the same pull through every sphere, and \(F\to1\) far away. The f dot dr derivation has the same three: the ruler of statement 4, the flux law with the count, and the far clock. There the algebra merges the two completely, with one solution, \(F=1-L/r\) and \(d\ell=dr/f\).

Where the two part: the exact differences

Three of Einstein's assumptions are not taken over (Section 6, statement 3). For each, here are general relativity's premise, the exact algebra, and the observation that could decide. Where the two disagree, the burden is on general relativity's premise.

1. One metric for every observer, continued across \(L\)

General relativity's premise (A1, and its step at \(L\)). Every observer measures components of one and the same metric, at every distance. When the solution for one body is continued across \(L\), general relativity takes one metric for all observers and continues the same solution, with the same mass, on the other side (Section 9). Nothing outside \(L\) requires that step. It is the step by which general relativity merges the frames' different values into one event horizon, crossed one way only, and it is the error that the paper names (Section 18).

The exact algebra. Continued across \(L\), the clock factor \(F=1-L/r\) is negative inside. A frame held at rest would have the four-velocity \(u=\alpha\,\partial_t\) with \(g(u,u)=-c^2\), so \(\alpha^2F=1\), and where \(F\lt0\) no real \(\alpha\) exists: the continued solution has no frame held at rest inside \(L\) (Section 9). Its law of free motion is carried inside unchanged:

\[ \Big(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\Big)^2=c^2\Big(e^2-1+\frac Lr\Big)\qquad\text{(the continued solution)}. \]

In the f dot dr derivation, premises 7 and 8 stand in that place (Section 3). Between two frames at rest, the factor that converts one frame's reading into the other's is \(s_{PQ}=f_Q/f_P\), with \(s_{PQ}^2=(1-L/r_Q)/(1-L/r_P)\). Across \(L\) the square is negative:

\[ s_{PQ}=\pm\,i\,\sqrt{\frac{L/r_Q-1}{1-L/r_P}}\,: \]

two solutions, and they are complex. What each frame reads is real for that frame. A body in free motion keeps its count \(e\), and one law holds on both sides of \(L\):

\[ \Big(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\Big)^2=c^2\Big(e^2-\Big|1-\frac Lr\Big|\Big). \]

Outside \(L\) the two laws are one. Inside, \(|1-L/r|\) stands where the continued solution has \(1-L/r\), and the two squared speeds differ by exactly \(2c^2(L/r-1)\). Inside \(L\) the pull on the body's own clock points outward, \(d^2r/d\tau^2=+G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/r^2\). A body that falls in passes \(L\) at the speed \(c\,e\) on its own clock, in a finite time of its own. It turns at \(L/(1+e^2)\), where the continued solution still has it moving inward with \((dr/d\tau)^2=2c^2e^2\), and it comes back out through \(L\) with its count \(e\), gaining the speed for the way out on the journey out itself, from the outward pull. An observer at rest far away cannot and does not observe the passage: on that observer's clock it has no finite time.

At \(L\) the clock rate of a frame held at rest is zero and the escape velocity is \(c\): \(L\) is the causal boundary, which is also the event horizon, of a frame at rest far away. The causal boundary depends on the frame of reference. At \(L\), the speed that a frame still needs in order to escape is

\[ c\ \ \text{(held at rest)},\qquad c\,\frac{1-e^2}{1+e^2}\ \ \text{(in free motion with count } e\le1\text{, falling in or on its way out, along its motion)}, \]

and none at all for \(e\ge1\). For \(e=0.5\) it is \(0.6\,c\). The author's picture for a boundary like this is the end of a rainbow: "It keeps moving and you never reach it."

The observation that could decide: anything, light or matter, arriving outside from inside an event horizon (Section 18). No observation has yet been made at or inside an event horizon, and nothing outside \(L\) requires general relativity's step. The burden is on that step.

2. Equations that are not linear: deficits that add, rates that multiply

General relativity's premise (A9). The class of equations that Einstein chose: built from the metric alone, of second order, with a divergence that vanishes identically. They are not linear: for point masses at different places their solution is not the sum of the solutions for each.

The exact algebra. In the f dot dr derivation the counted pull of every point mass is Newton's law in the frame of the calculation, the pulls add, and so do the squared-clock deficits, exactly, with the pull counted through the areas of that frame's grid (statement 7 and its premise table). In Einstein's equations, for bodies at rest on one line and held apart, the logarithms of the clock rates add in Weyl's coordinates, so his clock rates multiply (Weyl's solutions, with each body's rate taken in his coordinates). Write \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) for the two deficits \(1-f^2\):

\[ F_{\text{f dot dr}}=1-q_1-q_2,\qquad F_{\text{Einstein}}=(1-q_1)(1-q_2)=1-q_1-q_2+q_1q_2, \]
\[ \boxed{\;F_{\text{Einstein}}-F_{\text{f dot dr}}=q_1\,q_2\;} \]

For the same two deficits the two give the same clocks to first order and part at the second, by the product of the two deficits: for the deficits 0.25 and 0.0833 (the deficits at the place 4000 of the two-body example), the squared clock rate is 0.6667 here and 0.6875 there. Light that leaves such a place is redshifted by 22.5 percent here against 20.6 percent there; with the deficits 0.3 and 0.3, by 58.1 against 42.9 percent (Section 17). The f dot dr redshift is the higher, always. To compare the two at one and the same place, named by measurements, takes the rulers between two bodies, which are not yet stated (Section 17).

The rule for the rulers between two bodies, with the ruler, the scale and the clock updated to the frame of the calculation, is not yet stated in Section 4, and nothing is claimed about it here (Section 6).

Inside a body. A body is an arrangement of many counts, so the same parting reaches inside it (Section 17). For a round body the sum needs only the spheres around the centre. The flux law through the sphere at the radius \(s\), with the ruler along the pull at \(f\) (premise 5 inside matter, which statement 4 gives there as it does outside), gives

\[ \frac{dF}{ds}=\frac{\ell(s)}{s^2},\qquad F(s)=1-\frac{L}{2R}\Big(3-\frac{s^2}{R^2}\Big)\ \ \text{for a count spread evenly}, \]

with \(\ell(s)\) the count inside \(s\), as a length, and \(R\) the body's radius. General relativity's solution for its own evenly spread body (comparison side) has \(f(s)=\tfrac32\sqrt{1-L/R}-\tfrac12\sqrt{1-Ls^2/R^3}\). The exact difference is

\[ f_{\mathrm{GR}}^2-F=\tfrac34\Big(\sqrt{1-Ls^2/R^3}-\sqrt{1-L/R}\Big)^2\ \ge0 , \]

so wherever both clock rates are real, the f dot dr clock is the slower. For the neutron star of 1.4 suns and 12 km, a clock at the centre is redshifted by 43.9 percent here and by 40.0 percent in general relativity's solution; for 2.08 suns and 12.4 km, by 97.3 against 76.8 percent. Here the ruler inside the matter is premise 5. General relativity's interior ruler is \(ds/d\ell=\sqrt{1-\ell(s)/s}\), the one its equation (37) sets (line 4 above); with it the clock inside becomes general relativity's interior solution, and in the paper's words that ruler "is not a repair of this derivation but the premise under comparison" (Section 17). With the deficits adding, the clock at the centre of an evenly spread body at rest reaches zero, as read far away, when \(R=1.5\,L\), with the light from its surface redshifted by 73.2 percent; in general relativity's solution that happens at \(R=1.125\,L\), at 200 percent (Section 17).

The observation that could decide: a measurement of clock rates between two bodies, once the place between them is named by measurements, and inside one (Section 4, statement 10; Section 10, Part 16). The burden is on the class of equations Einstein chose (A9), and on the premise that his equations hold inside matter at such redshifts, which no observation has tested (Section 17).

3. Changes that move at the speed of light

General relativity's premise (A4, A9, A10). Its equations go on holding while the arrangement changes. To first order (A19) they then give a wave equation for a change of the metric,

\[ \Big(\nabla^2-\frac1{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\Big)\bar q_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{16\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}, \]

and a wave of such change moves at the speed \(c\) (Section 5, steps 13d and 13e). General relativity reads such a wave as carrying energy and momentum away, while Section 5 itself gives the pattern of clocks and rulers no local density of energy (part 12).

The exact algebra. In its place stands premise 6: "The rulers are updated instantaneously (not transferred via information carried by light and at the speed of light)." The same sum holds at every moment \(T\), with each body's place and count at that moment:

\[ F(\mathbf x,T)=1-\sum_a\frac{L_a(T)}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_a(T)|},\qquad \nabla^2F=0\ \ \text{between the bodies, at every moment } T. \]

In the equation for the clock, to first order, the difference is one term, \(-(1/c^2)\,\partial^2/\partial t^2\). Einstein's first-order equations carry it, and it is what gives a change of his metric the speed \(c\). The sum at every moment has no such term: the pull on each body points to where the others are at that moment, the pulls on two bodies are equal and opposite, and the total momentum is constant, exactly, \(d\mathbf P/dT=\sum_i\mathbf F_i(T)=0\). Premise 6 also says why light cannot carry the change: if it had to, the source would send energy out without end, the count inside any surface around it would fall, and with it the pull, against premise 1 (Section 3).

The observations that could decide: whether an instrument takes up energy, or recoils, when a count far from it moves, with no light from the event; and when an instrument registers the change, compared with the light from the same event (Section 6). Premise 6 says what is seen: the change is at once, and what an instrument registers comes with the light, so a delay that is observed is the light's (Section 6). The mathematics of what a given instrument registers is still to be written in the paper. The burden is on general relativity's premise that its equations go on holding while the arrangement changes.

Do the two say the same thing?

The algebra above answers in two parts.

Around one body at rest, outside \(L\), they say the same thing, differently. The same clock \(F=1-L/r\), the same ruler \(d\ell=dr/f\), the same four parts of the curvature, the same invariant \(12L^2/r^6\), and \(R_{\mu\nu}=0\). Einstein's route assumes one metric for every observer, a class of equations and an arrangement that never changes; it gets \(NB=1\) from two field equations and the mass as a constant fixed far away. The f dot dr route gets the ruler from equating two integrals of f dot dr, the pull through every sphere from the count, and the mass as the complete count. The agreement does not confirm the field equations. It shows that the measurements made so far around one body cannot tell the ways apart (Section 12): the tests of the redshift made so far are passed by both; the orbits and the paths of light are not yet derived from Section 4 (Section 18). The premise of general relativity it touches is that the clocks and rulers around a body are fixed by its field equations; here they come from the count, with no field equation (Section 12).

In three places they say different things, by exact amounts, and in each the burden is on a premise of general relativity.

  • For several bodies, \(q_1q_2\) in the squared clock rate for the same two deficits, and a slower clock inside a body. The premise: the class of equations that Einstein chose (A9), and his equations inside matter. What could decide: clock rates measured between two bodies, once the place between them is named by measurements, and inside one.
  • When the count moves, the sum at every moment against a wave with the speed \(c\). The premise: that his equations go on holding while the arrangement changes (A4, A9, A10). What could decide: whether an instrument takes up energy, or recoils, when a count far from it moves, with no light from the event.
  • At \(L\) and beyond, premises 7 and 8 against one metric continued for every observer. The premise: that continuation, the step that merges the frames' different values into one event horizon, crossed one way only. What could decide: anything, light or matter, arriving outside from inside an event horizon.

So the two say the same thing, differently, around one body at rest, outside \(L\). In the three places above they say different things, and there the burden is on general relativity's premises.
