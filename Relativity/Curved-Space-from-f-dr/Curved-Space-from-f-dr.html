Curved Space from f · dr

The curvature of space derived from f · dr, the mistake in Einstein's derivation (a hidden static constant), its correction line by line, and the observations at high redshift.

An article of the section Relativity of Physics: Particle Reconstruction and General Relativity, written for teachers and curious readers. It comes from the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy.

This page derives the curvature of space from one idea, the integral of f · dr, and uses it to find and correct the mistake in Einstein's derivation of general relativity.

The mistake is in Einstein's derivation. It puts a hidden static constant where a function of the frame belongs: one mass, fixed far away, and one map of clocks and rulers for every observer, in place of the mass as each frame reads it and each frame's own clock and ruler.

Around one body at rest, outside its event horizon, the two derivations give exactly the same clocks and rulers, so the mistake is hard to spot. They part inside the event horizon, where more than one body pulls, and when bodies move. Section 5 of this page corrects Einstein's derivation line by line.

1. A Thought Experiment, and What f · dr Means

Here is the thought experiment at the heart of the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy (Section 1). References like (Section 6, statement 3) are to that paper.

Seal a heavy body inside a closed surface, like a jar with its lid on: nothing gets in, nothing gets out. Put an observer, A, outside it, far away. List every kind of energy inside the jar (rest energy, energy of motion, potential energy, heat, light), each once, in A's units. The paper calls this total the count. Divided by \(c^2\), the square of the speed of light, it is the jar's mass, \(\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}\). The count sets the pull of the jar on A. With the lid on, the count cannot change, and so the pull cannot either. In the author's words, premise 1: "The gravitational force between two Gaussian surfaces is time invariant."

Now let a stone fall inside the jar, past a row of observers held at rest on platforms at different heights. Each of them reads the passing stone's energy as \(\gamma m_0c^2\), where \(m_0\) is the stone's rest mass and \(\gamma\) is special relativity's factor for its speed (premise 2). Lower down the stone is faster, so the reading grows. The stone gets heavier as it falls.

A total that cannot change, and a reading that grows. Both hold in one way only: the clock of the observer held where the stone is runs slower, by exactly the factor by which the reading has grown (premise 3). Write \(f\) for that clock's rate, as a share of A's clock. Then the stone's entry in A's count, its reading times that clock rate, \(\gamma f\,m_0c^2\), never changes; the paper writes \(e=\gamma f\) for it. The potential energy is the clock rate of the observer held there, and the paper calls the ratio of the clock rates at two places the curvature between them (Section 3, premise 3; Section 4).

What f · dr means

In "f · dr" (the paper writes "f dot dr"), f is the force on the falling stone and dr a small step along its path, so f · dr is the work the force does over that step. Everywhere else on this page \(f\) is the clock rate. The method, in the author's words (Section 4):

  • "Figure out the integral of f dot dr."
  • "Figure out how much energy is gained. Get the mass equivalent and divide it by the mass."
  • "The curvature between the two points will be the same as the ratio in (2) above."

In plain words: turn the work of each small step into mass, divide by the mass, and add up along the way. The pull acts on the stone's mass as it is at that moment, the energy already gained included, so the gains compound, like interest on interest. Added up, they give one equation:

\[ \frac{f_1}{f_2}=\frac{m_2}{m_1}=\exp\!\left(\int_1^2\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2}\right). \]

Here \(\mathbf g\) is the pull per unit of mass, \(d\boldsymbol\ell\) a small step measured with the ruler of the observer held there, \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) the stone's masses at the two places, and \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) the clock rates of the observers held there. The stone gets heavier by exactly the factor by which the clock slows.

With numbers (Section 4, statement 6). Write the count as a length, \(L=2G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/c^2\), with \(G\) Newton's constant, and name each place by \(r\), the circumference of a circle around the body divided by \(2\pi\). Take \(L=1000\) and let the stone go at rest at \(r=2000\). By \(r=1100\) the integral, divided by \(c^2\), is 0.852374, and \(\exp(0.852374)=2.34521\). The stone is 2.34521 times as heavy as when it was let go, and the clock held at 1100 runs at 0.30151 of A's clock, against 0.70711 at 2000: slower by the same factor. Hold the mass fixed instead and the same integral gives \(1+0.852374=1.852374\), and the count does not close.

The same jar, with a charge

Now take the heavy body out of the jar. In its place put a metal ball with a positive charge, held where it is, and let an electron go at rest far from the ball, still inside the jar.

The ball's charge pulls the electron in, and f · dr works as before, now with the electric force \(\mathbf F\): the work of each step becomes energy of motion, and the mass of that energy is the work divided by \(c^2\) (Section 4, statement 4: \(\ln\gamma=\int\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell/(mc^2)\)). The electron gets heavier as it falls. The jar, again, cannot. What pays this time is the electrostatic potential energy \(U\) of the ball and the electron: it drops by exactly what the energy of motion \(K\) gains, \(dK=\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=-dU\) (Section 2). That stored energy had its mass, \(U/c^2\), in the count all along. Through every closed surface around both, the two bodies pull as one point mass, \(\mathcal M=(E_1+E_2+U_{12})/c^2\), with \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) their counts and \(U_{12}\) the potential energy between them (Section 4, statement 5); close to them, the pull at a given place is not that of one point mass but the pulls of the pieces added, with \(U_{12}\) counted once among them (Section 4, statement 7). As the electron falls, \(U_{12}\) drops and the electron's count rises by the same amount. So the count does not change, and neither does the pull of the jar, nor the clocks and rulers that the pull sets.

The paper builds a machine that does just that with two charges near a heavy body, and states the way out: "Stored energy has mass before it becomes motion, and when it becomes motion, the motion's mass is exactly the mass the stored energy gives up. Every kind of potential energy

The falling stone and the falling electron are one thought experiment with two different stores. Premise 3 covers both: "Any potential energy (gravitational potential, electrostatic potential) must curve space so that when the potential energy is released, the curvature changes to nullify the increase in kinetic energy." So every kind of potential energy that can turn into energy of motion is in the count, each once (Section 2): gravitational potential energy, which in this paper is the clock rate \(f\) of the frame held at rest; electrostatic potential energy; magnetic interaction energy; nuclear binding energy, already inside a measured nuclear mass and not added again; the energy a weak decay releases; scalar potential energy, the Higgs value included; chemical bond energy; elastic strain energy (a spring stretched by \(x\) holds \(\tfrac12kx^2\), which is \(kx^2/2c^2\) of extra mass); and surface energy. For a spinning body the count takes the mass equivalent of all the potential energy that could be converted into kinetic energy (Section 4, the physicist's box).

2. The Derivation

The derivation in three versions: for a child, for a high school physics teacher, and in full. The full one is in the closed box at the end of this section; click its title to open it. All three use the same few things: the conservation of mass-energy and of momentum, the paper's premises, special relativity at one place, Planck's relation, and Newton's law far from the mass, which fixes \(G\) (Section 4). None of them uses an equation of Einstein's, or one map of clocks and rulers shared by every observer.

For a child

A ball falls toward a heavy body and speeds up. Its extra energy has mass, so the ball gets a little heavier. But the box around everything is sealed, so the total inside can't change, and neither can the pull of the box. Something has to make up for the ball's extra mass, exactly. It is the clock where the ball is: it runs slower, by exactly the share the ball got heavier. Do that one small step at a time, all the way down, and you get how fast every clock runs at every place around the body.

Now picture two people at the same place near the body. One is held there, standing on a platform. The other is falling past. Nobody holds the one who falls, so that one keeps the clock and the ruler they started with. How fast is the faller going? Exactly as fast as if someone had pushed them, in empty space, with the same push over the same distance that the pull gave them on the way down. So the two are simply moving past each other, and for two people who move past each other we already know what happens: the clock changes, and the ruler along the motion changes, and the rulers across it stay the same. That gives the held person's clock and rulers.

Last, picture a bag with anything in it: stones, springs wound up tight, things flying around. From outside the bag you cannot tell what is in it. You can only tell the total, and the total pulls on you like one small heavy point. Wound-up springs count too: their stored energy is part of the total. Many bags are many points, and their effects on your clock simply add. If the bags move, you use where each one is right now.

For a high school physics teacher

The three steps, for a body that moves a small step \(d\boldsymbol\ell\) along the pull \(\mathbf g\), the pull per unit of mass:

  • Step 1, the work. The pull does the work \(dW=m\,\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\) on the body, where \(m\) is its mass at that moment.
  • Step 2, the mass of the energy gained, over the mass. The work becomes the body's energy, so its mass grows by \(dm=dW/c^2\). Divided by the mass, \(dm/m=\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell/c^2\). Added up over the small steps, \(\ln(m_B/m_A)=\int_A^B\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell/c^2\).
  • Step 3, the curvature. The clock rate of a frame held at rest falls by the same ratio: \(f_A/f_B=m_B/m_A\). So \(mf\), the body's energy as A counts it, never changes.

Far from the mass, \(\mathbf g\) is Newton's pull, and the clock rate is \(f\approx1-G\mathcal M/(c^2r)\): the familiar gravitational redshift.

From frames held at rest to free fall. The same work, done by a force on a body in empty space, gives the same mass ratio: \(\ln\gamma=\int\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell/(mc^2)\) is the work-energy theorem of special relativity. So the frame that falls from rest far away passes the held frame at \(\gamma=1/f\). The falling frame needs no force and keeps A's clock and rulers. Special relativity then gives the held frame's: its clock at \(1/\gamma=f\) of A's, its ruler along the force at \(f\) of A's, and its sideways rulers unchanged. A step \(dr\) on A's ruler reads \(d\ell=dr/f\) on the held frame's ruler.

Anything is a point mass. Gauss's law for the pull: outside a closed surface only the total inside counts, \(\mathcal M=E/c^2\), with every kind of energy in \(E\). Potential energy counts with its mass equivalent \(U/c^2\), which is negative for a bound pair.

One point mass. Count the pull through a sphere around the body, in A's units: \(4\pi r^2\,f\,g_{\mathrm{hold}}=4\pi G\mathcal M\), where \(g_{\mathrm{hold}}\) is the acceleration that holds a frame at rest there. Step 3 gives \(g_{\mathrm{hold}}=c^2\,d\ln f/d\ell\), and with \(d\ell=dr/f\) that makes \(f\,g_{\mathrm{hold}}=\tfrac12c^2\,d(f^2)/dr\). So \(r^2\,d(f^2)/dr=L\), and with \(f=1\) far away,

\[ f^2=1-\frac{2G\mathcal M}{c^2r}=1-\frac Lr . \]

In A's units the counted pull is Newton's, \(G\mathcal M/r^2\), exactly, at every strength. A held frame reads the body at \(\mathcal M/f\), heavier than A does: at twice \(L\) it reads 1.414 times A's value.

Many point masses. The counted pulls add as in Newton's theory, so \(f^2=1-\sum_a2G\mathcal M_a/(c^2r_a)\), with \(r_a\) the distance to the body \(a\). Where two pulls cancel, the clock still runs slow by both deficits \(1-f^2\).

Bodies in motion. A moving body's count includes its energy of motion. At every moment the same sum holds with the bodies' places at that moment, and the pull on each points to where the others are then, which is what keeps the total momentum constant.

The solution at a glance

For the frames held at rest around a body, outside \(L\), the clock rate, the ruler along the radius and the space that those frames measure are

\[ f=\sqrt{1-\frac Lr},\qquad d\ell=\frac{dr}{f},\qquad d\ell^2=\frac{dr^2}{1-L/r}+r^2\,d\Omega^2 , \]

with \(d\Omega^2=d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\) for the angles around the body. That space is curved, like a saddle in the planes that hold the radius and like a ball in the plane of the sphere:

\[ K_{r\theta}=K_{r\varphi}=-\frac{L}{2r^3},\qquad K_{\theta\varphi}=+\frac{L}{r^3}. \]

For many bodies the clocks are \(f^2=1-\sum_aL_a/|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_a|\), with \(L_a=2G\mathcal M_a/c^2\) and \(\mathbf x_a\) the place of the body \(a\). With the clock added, the first line is general relativity's solution outside a round body, reached here with no equation of Einstein's.

The full derivation

What goes in, and the symbols

The derivation uses only these (Section 4):

  1. The conservation of mass-energy and of momentum (Section 2). Draw a closed surface and let nothing cross it: the complete energy inside, every kind counted once in one observer's units, does not change. The paper calls it the count. Its mass equivalent is \(\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}=E_{\mathrm{enc}}/c^2\).
  2. The paper's premises 1 to 6 (Section 3).
  3. Special relativity at one place, between two frames that pass each other there.
  4. Planck's relation, with one constant in every frame.
  5. Newton's law far from the mass, which fixes \(G\).

Step 1. The idea: f · dr

The method, in the author's words (Section 4):

  • "Figure out the integral of f dot dr."
  • "Figure out how much energy is gained. Get the mass equivalent and divide it by the mass."
  • "The curvature between the two points will be the same as the ratio in (2) above."

Here is the mathematics of the first two. A body of rest mass \(m_0\) falls freely past frames held at rest. A frame held at rest reads its energy as \(mc^2=\gamma m_0c^2\) (premise 2). Over a step \(d\boldsymbol\ell\) the pull \(\mathbf g\) does work on the body's mass as it is at that moment, energy already gained included:

\[ dW=m\,\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell,\qquad dm=\frac{dW}{c^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dm}{m}=\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2},\qquad \ln\frac{m_2}{m_1}=\int_1^2\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2}. \]

In words: turn the work into mass, divide by the mass, and add up along the way. The pull always acts on the current mass, so the small gains compound like interest, and the sum is a logarithm.

Step 2. The clock

Now the third step. The surface around everything is closed, so the count inside cannot change, and neither can its pull (premise 1). The body's gain must therefore be cancelled where it occurs (premise 3). What cancels it is the clock rate of the frame held at rest there, which falls by the same ratio:

\[ \frac{f_1}{f_2}=\frac{m_2}{m_1}=\exp\!\left(\int_1^2\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2}\right), \qquad d\ln f=\frac{g_{\mathrm{hold}}\,d\ell}{c^2},\qquad \gamma f=e\ \ \text{constant}. \]

In words: the falling body gets heavier by exactly the share by which the local clock runs slower. Counted in A's units, its energy \(e\,m_0c^2\) never changes. This ratio of clock rates is what the paper means by the curvature between two points.

Three readings follow (statement 1):

  • an energy that the held frame reads as \(E\) counts as \(fE\) in A's units, so its unit of energy is \(f\) of A's;
  • a clock counts the ticks of a transition, and with the same Planck's constant the held frame's clock runs at \(f\) of A's;
  • light that climbs from the held frame to A is redshifted by \(1+z=1/f\).

Nothing in these steps asks what makes the pull. They hold between any two points, for any pull.

Step 3. The ruler

How fast is the falling body going as it passes a frame held at rest? The paper answers by equating two integrals of f · dr (statement 4).

Around the body, for a fall from rest far away, the three steps give

\[ \ln\gamma=\int_\infty^{r}\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2}=\ln\frac1f . \]

In empty space, a force \(\mathbf F\) that sets a body in motion does the same kind of work on its current mass, and special relativity gives the same ratio for the same integral:

\[ \frac{dm}{m}=\frac{\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{mc^2},\qquad \ln\gamma=\int\frac{\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{mc^2}. \]

Set the two equal. The falling frame is in exactly the motion that the same f · dr would generate there:

\[ \gamma=\frac1f,\qquad v=c\sqrt{1-f^2}. \]

The falling frame needs no force. No work is done on it, so it keeps A's clock, rulers and mass (premise 4). The two frames simply move past each other, and special relativity between them, at that place, changes the clock and the ruler along the motion and leaves the sideways rulers alone. So the frame held at rest has

\[ \text{its clock at } f \text{ of A's},\qquad \text{its ruler along the force at } f \text{ of A's},\qquad \text{its sideways rulers the same as A's}, \]

and a step \(dr\) along the force, on A's ruler, reads on the held frame's own ruler

\[ d\ell=\frac{dr}{f}. \]

In words: picture two people at the same place, one standing on a platform, one falling past. The faller is going exactly as fast as a push in empty space with the same f · dr would make them go. For two people who move past each other the answer is already known, and it gives the standing person's clock and ruler.

Step 4. The pull through every sphere: the clock rate

The count sets the pull through every sphere around a point mass (premise 1). Counted in A's units the pull is \(f\,g_{\mathrm{hold}}\), so

\[ 4\pi r^2\,f\,g_{\mathrm{hold}}=4\pi G\,\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}} . \]

Put in the clock step and the ruler. Since \(d\ell=dr/f\),

\[ g_{\mathrm{hold}}=c^2\,\frac{d\ln f}{d\ell}=c^2\,\frac{df}{dr},\qquad f\,g_{\mathrm{hold}}=c^2f\,\frac{df}{dr}=\frac{c^2}{2}\,\frac{dF}{dr}, \]

and the flux law becomes

\[ \frac{c^2}{2}\,\frac{dF}{dr}=\frac{G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}}{r^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2\,\frac{dF}{dr}=L \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;F=f^2=1-\frac Lr,\qquad L=\frac{2G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}}{c^2}\;} \]

with the clock rate set to 1 far away, where clocks agree with A's.

In words: counted in A's units, the pull is Newton's law, exactly, at every strength. The factor \(1/f\) in the ruler cancels the factor \(f\) in the counting.

The same result, written as f · dr itself: between any two places the square of the clock rate changes by twice the integral of the counted force over the path, per unit of the probe's rest energy \(\mu c^2\),

\[ F(r_2)-F(r_1)=\frac{2}{\mu c^2}\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\frac{G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}\,\mu}{r^2}\,dr . \]

Two things come free (statement 6). The falling frame passes the held one at \(v=c\sqrt{1-f^2}=\sqrt{2G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/r}\), Newton's speed of fall. And on its own clock the falling body obeys Newton's law exactly: \((dr/d\tau)^2=c^2(e^2-F)\), so \(d^2r/d\tau^2=-G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/r^2\).

At \(r=L\) the clock rate of a frame held at rest is zero: a zero, not a singularity. Everything in this derivation is for the outside of \(L\). What holds at \(L\) and beyond comes from premises 7 and 8, not from carrying any formula there (statement 6); section 6 below takes it up.

Step 5. The space the held frames measure

Along the radius, a held frame measures \(d\ell=dr/f\). Around the body its rulers are A's, so a circle has the circumference \(2\pi r\) for both. The space that the frames held at rest measure is therefore

\[ \boxed{\;d\ell^2=\frac{dr^2}{f^2}+r^2\,d\Omega^2=\frac{dr^2}{1-L/r}+r^2\big(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\big)\;} \]

With the clock added, the readings of the frames held at rest are

\[ ds^2=-F\,c^2dt^2+\frac{dr^2}{F}+r^2\,d\Omega^2 , \]

which is general relativity's solution outside a round body (Section 7), reached here with no equation of Einstein's. The frames that fall freely from rest far away read the same clocks and rulers as

\[ ds^2=-c^2dT^2+(dr+v\,dT)^2+r^2\,d\Omega^2,\qquad dT=dt+\frac{v}{c^2F}\,dr , \]

with A's time \(T\) and flat space (premise 4). Outside \(L\) the two say the same thing. So "the curvature of space" below means the curvature of the space that the frames held at rest measure. The frames that fall measure flat space, and each reading is real for the frame that makes it (premise 7).

Step 6. The curvature of that space, from the definition

Write a prime for the change per unit of distance on the held ruler, \(d/d\ell\). The ruler gives \(r'=dr/d\ell=f\). Differentiating once more, with \(f^2=1-L/r\),

\[ r''=\frac{df}{d\ell}=f\,\frac{df}{dr}=\frac12\,\frac{dF}{dr}=\frac{L}{2r^2}. \]

The planes that hold the radius. Cut space through the centre, for example along the equator. On that surface lengths are \(d\ell^2+r(\ell)^2\,d\varphi^2\), and Gauss's curvature of such a surface is

\[ K_{r\varphi}=-\frac{r''}{r}=-\frac{L}{2r^3}\qquad(\text{and }K_{r\theta}\text{ the same}). \]

In words: on a flat sheet the circles around a point grow at a steady rate, \(r''=0\). Here they grow faster and faster on the way out, \(r''\gt0\), so the surface curves like a saddle, and its curvature is negative.

The plane of the sphere. A sphere of radius \(r\) has the curvature \(1/r^2\) on its own. The space around it subtracts the square of the rate at which the spheres grow outward, \((r'/r)^2\) (Gauss's equation):

\[ K_{\theta\varphi}=\frac1{r^2}-\Big(\frac{r'}{r}\Big)^2=\frac{1-r'^2}{r^2}=\frac{1-F}{r^2}=+\frac{L}{r^3}. \]

In flat space \(r'=1\) and the two terms cancel. Here \(r'=f\lt1\): the spheres grow more slowly than in flat space, and what is left is \(L/r^3\).

The same, from the definition written in full (Section 10, Part 8, its part in space; the definition of curvature is mathematics). With \(\ell,\theta,\varphi\) as coordinates, so that \(d\ell^2+r^2d\theta^2+r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\), the connection coefficients are

\[ \Gamma^\ell{}_{\theta\theta}=-rr',\quad \Gamma^\ell{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-rr'\sin^2\theta,\quad \Gamma^\theta{}_{\ell\theta}=\Gamma^\varphi{}_{\ell\varphi}=\frac{r'}{r},\quad \Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta,\quad \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi}=\cot\theta , \]

and the curvature

\[ R^\alpha{}_{\beta\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\nu\beta}-\partial_\nu\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta}+\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\beta}-\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\beta} \]

gives

\[ R^\theta{}_{\ell\theta\ell}=-\partial_\ell\Big(\frac{r'}{r}\Big)-\Big(\frac{r'}{r}\Big)^2=-\frac{r''}{r},\qquad R^\theta{}_{\varphi\theta\varphi}=(1-r'^2)\sin^2\theta . \]

Divided by the squared unit lengths, these are \(K_{r\theta}=-r''/r\) and \(K_{\theta\varphi}=(1-r'^2)/r^2\).

The curvature of the clock rate. The same definition, applied to the clock rate, measures how \(f\) bends from place to place on the held rulers: \(C_n=(1/f)\,\partial^2f/\partial\ell_n^2\) along a straight line in the direction \(n\). Along the radius, \(f'=f\,df/dr=L/(2r^2)\) and \(f''=f\,d(f')/dr=-fL/r^3\). At a point of empty space it reads

\[ \boxed{\;F(\mathbf x)=f^2=1-\sum_a\frac{L_a}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_a|},\qquad L_a=\frac{2G\mathcal M_a}{c^2}\;} \]

\[ F(\mathbf x,T)=1-\sum_a\frac{L_a(T)}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_a(T)|},\qquad \frac{d\mathbf P}{dT}=\sum_i\mathbf F_i(T)=0 . \]

The pulls on two bodies are equal and opposite at every moment, so the total momentum never changes.

3. A Hidden Static Constant Instead of a Frame- or Reference-Dependent Function

The error in Einstein's derivation is a hidden static constant: one mass for the source, a number fixed far away and the same for every frame, and one metric that every observer reads, with one static clock \(t\) that never updates its rate (Section 5, assumptions A1, A15, A17 and A18; Section 7, part 9; the compact-notation page). What belongs in its place is a function of the frame: the count as each frame reads it, \(\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/f\), with each frame's own clock and ruler, updated by f · dr at every step (Section 4, statements 1, 2 and 4).

Outside the event horizon of one body at rest the solution already carries that function, since a frame held at rest there feels the pull \(G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/(r^2f)\), Newton's law with the mass it reads itself (Section 9, statement 2). The error shows inside the event horizon, where the static form is carried across \(L\) by a change of coordinates, every frame gets the far mass and one event horizon, and no frame is left at rest to read \(\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/f\) and keep the count (Section 9, statements 2 and 3; Section 18); and it shows where more than one count pulls: between bodies held apart, where the count's deficits add and Einstein's equations, which are not linear, multiply the clock rates of bodies at rest on one line (Section 1; Section 6, statement 3), and inside a body, where general relativity keeps one mass inside each sphere for every frame, and the f · dr derivation reads the count inside each sphere in the units of the frame of the calculation at that sphere (Section 17).

4. Why It Is Hard to Spot

Three things hide the error (Section 13).

Outside the event horizon the two derivations agree exactly. Around one body at rest they give the same clock, \(F=f^2=1-L/r\), the same ruler, \(d\ell=dr/f\), the same four parts of the curvature, the same invariant \(12L^2/r^6\), and \(R_{\mu\nu}=0\), Einstein's equations for empty space: they say the same thing, differently. Einstein's route assumes one metric for every observer, a class of equations and an arrangement that never changes; it gets \(NB=1\) (with \(N\) its clock and \(B\) its radial ruler, \(d\ell=B\,dr\)), the ruler that goes with the clock, from two field equations, and the mass as a constant fixed far away. The f · dr route gets the ruler from equating two integrals of f · dr, the pull through every sphere from the count, and the mass as the complete count. The agreement does not confirm the field equations. It shows that the measurements made so far around one body cannot tell the ways apart (Section 12): the confirmed tests of the redshift made so far, such as the white dwarf Sirius B, are passed by both, and far from the mass both give Newton's conserved energy, \(mc^2+\tfrac12mv^2-G\mathcal Mm/r\), which tests like Pound–Rebka and GPS clocks match (Section 13). The orbits and the paths of light are not yet derived from Section 4 (Section 18). The premise of general relativity this touches is that the clocks and rulers around a body are fixed by its field equations; here they come from the count, with no equation of Einstein's (Section 12).

The notation makes one frame's reading look like the count. In the compact line element \(ds^2=g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu\) the single symbol \(g_{\mu\nu}\) absorbs every adjustment of clocks and rulers, and the grid \((t,r)\) belongs to one fixed observer far away. Setting \(\partial_tg_{\mu\nu}=0\) locks everything to one clock \(t\) that never updates its rate. A photon's coordinate energy, \(E_\infty=-p_t\), then stays the same along its whole flight, and it looks as if nothing happened to it; unpacked, each observer held at rest reads it as \(E_\infty/f\) (the compact-notation page). In the same way, dividing by \(mc^2\) and treating \(e\) as a constant per unit of mass looks like routine unit normalization rather than taking one frame's reading for the count (Section 13). The paper's picture is a rocket: a formula that freezes the rocket's starting mass is right to half a percent when 1 percent of it burns as fuel, and wrong by a factor of 4.65 when 99 percent burns (Section 4, the teacher's box). Far from a body \(f\) is close to 1, and a frozen reading and the count agree. At the event horizon the reading \(\gamma m_0c^2\) grows without bound, while the body's entry in the count, \(\gamma f\,m_0c^2\), does not change (Section 4, statement 6).

No observation reaches inside an event horizon. The step that carries the static form across \(L\) comes fourteen parts into the derivation, by a change of coordinates (Section 7, parts 15 to 17), in a region where \(1-L/r\) changes sign and no direct measurement has been made. So it reads as routine algebra rather than as a premise (Section 13).

Einstein's field equations (the comparison side)

This is general relativity's own derivation, as the paper gives it in Section 5. It is the comparison side: nothing from the f · dr derivation is used in it. Among its twenty-one assumptions:

  • one spacetime with one metric, and every observer measures components of this same metric (A1);
  • the equivalence principle (A2), and ideal clocks and rulers that read the metric (A3);
  • equations of the same form in every coordinate system (A4);
  • matter entering through one stress-energy tensor \(T_{\mu\nu}\), conserved locally (A7, A8);
  • a geometric side built from the metric alone, of second order, with a divergence that vanishes identically (A9), and a source side that is one constant times \(T_{\mu\nu}\) (A10);
  • Newton's law far from the mass (A11), and a cosmological constant of zero (A12).

From these, with the constant fixed by Newton's law,

\[ R_{\mu\nu}-\frac12\,R\,g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu},\qquad R_{\mu\nu}=0\ \ \text{in empty space}. \]

For an arrangement at rest, write the metric with the clock rate \(f\) and the rulers \(h_{ij}\) of the frames at rest, \(ds^2=-f^2c^2dt^2+h_{ij}\,dx^idx^j\). With \(\varepsilon\) the energy density that the frame at rest reads, \(S_{ij}\) the stresses and \(S\) the sum of the three pressures, the equations become (Section 5, part 10)

\[ \begin{aligned} (35)\qquad&\Delta_hf=\frac{4\pi G}{c^4}\,f\,(\varepsilon+S),\\ (36)\qquad&R_{ij}(h)-\frac{D_iD_jf}{f}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\Big[S_{ij}+\tfrac12\,h_{ij}\,(\varepsilon-S)\Big],\\ (37)\qquad&R(h)=\frac{16\pi G}{c^4}\,\varepsilon . \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\Delta_hf=D_iD^if\) is the Laplacian of the clock rate on the rulers \(h_{ij}\), \(D_iD_jf\) are its second derivatives along those rulers, and \(R_{ij}(h)\) is the Ricci curvature of the space those rulers measure, with \(R(h)\) its sum over the three directions. The first is the time-time part, \(R_{00}=f\,\Delta_hf\). The second holds the parts in space. The third is the time-time part of the Einstein tensor, \(G_{00}/f^2=R(h)/2\).

To first order, for an arrangement that changes (A19), the equations become a wave equation for \(\bar q_{\mu\nu}\), the first-order change of the metric, trace-reversed, with the speed \(c\) (part 13):

\[ \Box\,\bar q_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{16\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu},\qquad \Box=\nabla^2-\frac1{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}. \]

6. Where the Results Part, and the Observations at High Redshift

Three of Einstein's assumptions are not taken over (Section 6, statement 3). For each, here are general relativity's premise and the exact algebra. Where the two disagree, the burden is on general relativity's premise. The last part sets the calculations of the redshift beside the observations with the highest redshifts.

1. One metric for every observer, continued across \(L\)

General relativity's premise (A1, and its step at \(L\)). Every observer measures components of one and the same metric, at every distance. When the solution for one body is continued across \(L\), general relativity takes one metric for all observers and continues the same solution, with the same mass, on the other side (Section 9). Nothing outside \(L\) requires that step. It is the step by which general relativity merges the frames' different values into one event horizon, crossed one way only, and it is the error that the paper names (Section 18).

The exact algebra. Continued across \(L\), the clock factor \(F=1-L/r\) is negative inside. A frame held at rest would have the four-velocity \(u=\alpha\,\partial_t\) with \(g(u,u)=-c^2\), so \(\alpha^2F=1\), and where \(F\lt0\) no real \(\alpha\) exists: the continued solution has no frame held at rest inside \(L\) (Section 9). Its law of free motion is carried inside unchanged:

\[ \Big(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\Big)^2=c^2\Big(e^2-1+\frac Lr\Big)\qquad\text{(the continued solution)}. \]

In the f · dr derivation, premises 7 and 8 stand in that place (Section 3). Between two frames at rest, the factor that converts one frame's reading into the other's is \(s_{PQ}=f_Q/f_P\), with \(s_{PQ}^2=(1-L/r_Q)/(1-L/r_P)\). Across \(L\) the square is negative:

\[ s_{PQ}=\pm\,i\,\sqrt{\frac{L/r_Q-1}{1-L/r_P}}\,: \]

two solutions, and they are complex. What each frame reads is real for that frame. A body in free motion keeps its count \(e\), and one law holds on both sides of \(L\):

\[ \Big(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\Big)^2=c^2\Big(e^2-\Big|1-\frac Lr\Big|\Big). \]

Outside \(L\) the two laws are one. Inside, \(|1-L/r|\) stands where the continued solution has \(1-L/r\), and the two squared speeds differ by exactly \(2c^2(L/r-1)\). Read by the frames held at rest there, with their ruler \(dr/\sqrt{L/r-1}\), the continued law gives the body the count \(\gamma\sqrt{L/r-1}=\sqrt{e^2+2(L/r-1)}\), which changes along the path, against premise 3 (Section 14, Part 7). Inside \(L\) the pull on the body's own clock points outward, \(d^2r/d\tau^2=+G\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}/r^2\). A body that falls in passes \(L\) at the speed \(c\,e\) on its own clock, in a finite time of its own. It turns at \(L/(1+e^2)\), where the continued solution still has it moving inward with \((dr/d\tau)^2=2c^2e^2\), and it comes back out through \(L\) with its count \(e\), gaining the speed for the way out on the journey out itself, from the outward pull. An observer at rest far away cannot and does not observe the passage: on that observer's clock it has no finite time.

At \(L\) the clock rate of a frame held at rest is zero and the escape velocity is \(c\): \(L\) is the causal boundary, which is also the event horizon, of a frame at rest far away. The causal boundary depends on the frame of reference. At \(L\), the speed that a frame still needs in order to escape is \(c\) for a frame held at rest. For a frame in free motion with count \(e\le1\), falling in or on its way out, it is, along its motion,

\[ c\,\frac{1-e^2}{1+e^2}, \]

and none at all for \(e\ge1\). For \(e=0.5\) it is \(0.6\,c\). The author's picture for a boundary like this is the end of a rainbow: "It keeps moving and you never reach it."

Nothing outside \(L\) requires general relativity's step there, and the burden is on that step.

2. Equations that are not linear: deficits that add, rates that multiply

General relativity's premise (A9). The class of equations that Einstein chose: built from the metric alone, of second order, with a divergence that vanishes identically. They are not linear: for point masses at different places their solution is not the sum of the solutions for each.

The exact algebra. In the f · dr derivation the counted pull of every point mass is Newton's law in the frame of the calculation, the pulls add, and so do the squared-clock deficits, exactly, with the pull counted through the areas of that frame's grid (statement 7 and its premise table). In Einstein's equations, for bodies at rest on one line and held apart, the logarithms of the clock rates add in Weyl's coordinates, so his clock rates multiply (Weyl's solutions, with each body's rate taken in his coordinates). Write \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) for the two deficits \(1-f^2\):

\[ F_{\text{f dot dr}}=1-q_1-q_2,\qquad F_{\text{Einstein}}=(1-q_1)(1-q_2)=1-q_1-q_2+q_1q_2, \]
\[ \boxed{\;F_{\text{Einstein}}-F_{\text{f dot dr}}=q_1\,q_2\;} \]

For the same two deficits the two give the same clocks to first order and part at the second, by the product of the two deficits: for the deficits 0.25 and 0.0833 (the deficits at the place 4000 of the two-body example of step 7), the squared clock rate is 0.6667 here and 0.6875 there. Light that leaves such a place is redshifted by 22.5 percent here against 20.6 percent there; with the deficits 0.3 and 0.3, by 58.1 against 42.9 percent (Section 17). The f · dr redshift is the higher, always. To compare the two at one and the same place, named by measurements, takes the rulers between two bodies, which are not yet stated (Section 17), and nothing is claimed about them here (Section 6).

Inside a body. A body is an arrangement of many counts, and the two derivations part inside it too (Section 17). For a round body the calculation needs only the spheres around the centre. The pull through each is set by the count inside it (premise 1), and the ruler along the pull is that of the frame of the calculation at that sphere, with the count inside read in that frame's units, as Section 4 updates the ruler, the scale and the clock near a body that sits in the pull of others, such as the Sun in the pull of the Galaxy (premise 5; Section 4). In symbols: \(\ell(s)\) is the count inside the sphere of radius \(s\), in A's units, as a length; \(R\) is the body's radius; and \(ds/d\ell\) is the ruler along the pull, a step \(ds\) in the radius of the circles reading \(d\ell\) on the held ruler (outside the body \(ds/d\ell=f\), as in step 3 of the full derivation). The frame of the calculation at the sphere has its clock at \(f/(ds/d\ell)\) of A's, so it reads the count inside at \(\ell\,(ds/d\ell)/f\) (Section 4, statement 2), and relative to it the frame held at the sphere has its clock and its ruler along the pull at the same factor, \(ds/d\ell\), as premise 5 gives them. The ruler is the one-body ruler of steps 3 and 4, \((ds/d\ell)^2=1-L/s\), with \(L\) replaced by that count, and the pull through the sphere, with the clock step, gives the clock whatever the ruler (Section 17):

\[ \Big(\frac{ds}{d\ell}\Big)^2=1-\frac{\ell(s)}{f\,s}\,\frac{ds}{d\ell},\qquad s^2\,\frac{df}{d\ell}=\frac{\ell(s)}{2}. \]

For a round body at rest whose count, in A's units per unit of grid volume (the volume \(4\pi s^2\,ds\) between the spheres at \(s\) and \(s+ds\)), is spread evenly, \(\ell(s)=L\,s^3/R^3\), the clock rate at the centre, as A reads it, is

\[ f(0)=\Big(1-\frac{5L}{4R}\Big)^{3/5}. \]

General relativity's solution for its own evenly spread body (comparison side) has \(f(s)=\tfrac32\sqrt{1-L/R}-\tfrac12\sqrt{1-Ls^2/R^3}\), and a faster clock at the centre, wherever both clock rates are real. The f · dr clock is the slower at every place inside the body where both are real, at every size: below the surface its ruler has \((ds/d\ell)^2\lt1-\ell(s)/s\), the ruler with which the same clock step gives general relativity's clock, so \(df/ds=\ell(s)/(2s^2\,ds/d\ell)\) is the larger on the way in from the same surface value. The two agree to the second order in \(L/R\) at every place and part at the third; to that order,

\[ f_{\mathrm{GR}}-f=\frac{L^3}{64R^3}\Big(1-\frac{s^2}{R^2}\Big)^2\Big(1+\frac{2s^2}{R^2}\Big), \]

which is \(L^3/64R^3\) at the centre. For the neutron star of 1.4 suns and 12 km, a clock at the centre is redshifted by 40.2 percent here and by 40.0 percent in general relativity's solution; for 2.08 suns and 12.4 km, by 78.5 against 76.8 percent.

For the even spread the step that parts the two is the count in the ruler. General relativity's interior ruler is \(ds/d\ell=\sqrt{1-\ell(s)/s}\), the one its equation (37) sets (line 4 of the algebra in section 5), and with it the same clock step gives general relativity's interior solution exactly. That ruler keeps A's scale in the count inside each sphere, where the f · dr derivation reads the count in the units of the frame of the calculation at that sphere. That is general relativity's premise of one mass inside each sphere for every frame, which Section 4, statement 2, denies (Section 17).

The count per unit of grid volume is not the density that the held frames read in their own units: that density is the count per unit of grid volume times \((ds/d\ell)/f\). For a body whose density, as the held frames read it, is the same everywhere, the clock at the centre is the faster one: a clock there is redshifted by 38.4 percent for 1.4 suns and 12 km, against 40.0 percent in general relativity's solution (Section 17).

With the ruler of each sphere updated, \(ds/d\ell\to1\) at the centre wherever the clock there is above zero: a small sphere at the distance \(d\) on the held rulers has the area \(4\pi d^2\), the curvature of space stays finite, and the centre is a regular point. A's ruler carried through every layer, \(ds/d\ell=f\), would make the centre a conical point, and inside an empty shell it would put a curvature of space where nothing pulls and no move stores or releases potential energy; premise 3, read at every place, makes space flat there, and so excludes that ruler (Section 17). The ruler is derived on two additions: premise 3 read at every place for the curvature of space, and Section 4's update of the ruler, the scale and the clock, taken to every sphere. Neither is yet derived from premises 1 to 6 for a filled body. What is not yet understood is why, inside matter, the plane of the sphere keeps the relation it has around one point mass between the curvature of space and the curvature of the clock rate, \(K_{\theta\varphi}=C_\theta+C_\varphi\); premise 3's mathematics carries the clock only, and the equation that carries its sentence for the ruler is not yet written (Section 17).

With the clock updated sphere by sphere, the clock at the centre of an evenly spread body at rest reaches zero, as read far away, when \(R=1.25\,L\), with the light from its surface redshifted by \(\sqrt5-1\), 123.6 percent. That is where the causal boundary of the observer far away, which is also the event horizon, first appears: at the centre of the body, while the surface is still outside \(L\). In general relativity's solution the clock rate at the centre reaches zero at \(R=1.125\,L\), at a surface redshift of 200 percent (Section 17).

That makes 123.6 percent a ceiling. A frame cannot be held at rest where its clock rate, as read far away, is zero while the pull is not: the force that would hold it has no finite value on its own scale, and the ceiling takes that as its premise, which is not among the eight premises of the paper's Section 3. The even spread is the extreme case: every other spread that is nowhere denser farther out has, at every place, a clock no faster than the even spread's, and its centre reaches zero at a larger radius (at \(1.32\,L\), \(1.46\,L\) and \(1.47\,L\) for the three spreads denser toward the centre that the paper computes). Below the radius at which its centre reaches zero, while the surface is still outside \(L\), the clock rate is zero on a sphere inside the body, where the pull is not zero, and a body at rest is filled with pieces held at rest, so one of them would sit on that sphere. So a round body at rest whose count, in A's units per unit of grid volume, is nowhere denser farther out than farther in, and whose surface lies outside \(L\), has \(R\ge1.25\,L\), and the light from its surface is redshifted by at most 123.6 percent, with equality only for the even spread. General relativity allows up to 200 percent (Buchdahl's limit, for a fluid with the same pressure in every direction, a finite pressure at the centre and none at the surface; Section 17).

The ceiling does not hold for a body whose count is denser farther out: inside a shell (Section 4) the clock rate is the same everywhere, and the redshift of its surface has no limit before \(R=L\). Nor does it hold for a body whose density, as the held frames read it, is the same everywhere: its count per unit of grid volume grows outward, its clock at the centre stays above zero down to \(R=L\), and there is no limit before \(R=L\). So above 200 percent the two part again: for a body at rest made of a fluid with the same pressure in every direction, whose energy density is nowhere greater farther out, with a finite pressure at the centre and none at the surface, general relativity's equations inside matter exclude a surface line above 200 percent (Buchdahl's limit), and the paper allows it (Section 17). Which spread a real star's count has is not yet known: whether there is a limit depends on it, it follows from what holds the body up, and observation can constrain it. The highest surface lines in the table below, 72 ± 5, 65 ± 3 and 64.2 ± 3.2 percent, need a radius of 1.51, 1.58 and 1.59 times \(L\), so all three leave room for a round body at rest whose count is nowhere denser farther out: the even spread reaches the causal boundary at its centre only at \(1.25\,L\), and the three spreads denser toward the centre at \(1.32\,L\) to \(1.47\,L\). A spread still denser toward the centre reaches the causal boundary at its centre beyond \(1.511\,L\) (at \(1.56\,L\) for a count density ten times higher inside \(R/4\) than outside it), so if one of these lines is confirmed as a surface line, the spread of the star's count, measured in some other way, says where the star's centre is (Section 17).

The burden is on the class of equations Einstein chose (A9), and on the premise that his equations hold inside matter at such redshifts, which no observation has tested (Section 17).

3. Changes that move at the speed of light

General relativity's premise (A4, A9, A10). Its equations go on holding while the arrangement changes. To first order (A19) they then give a wave equation for a change of the metric,

\[ \Big(\nabla^2-\frac1{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\Big)\bar q_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{16\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}, \]

and a wave of such change moves at the speed \(c\) (Section 5, steps 13d and 13e). General relativity reads such a wave as carrying energy and momentum away, while Section 5 itself gives the pattern of clocks and rulers no local density of energy (part 12).

The exact algebra. In its place stands premise 6: "The rulers are updated instantaneously (not transferred via information carried by light and at the speed of light)." The same sum holds at every moment \(T\), with each body's place and count at that moment:

\[ F(\mathbf x,T)=1-\sum_a\frac{L_a(T)}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_a(T)|},\qquad \nabla^2F=0\ \ \text{between the bodies, at every moment } T. \]

In the equation for the clock, to first order, the difference is one term, \(-(1/c^2)\,\partial^2/\partial t^2\). Einstein's first-order equations carry it, and it is what gives a change of his metric the speed \(c\). The sum at every moment has no such term: the pull on each body points to where the others are at that moment, the pulls on two bodies are equal and opposite, and the total momentum is constant, exactly, \(d\mathbf P/dT=\sum_i\mathbf F_i(T)=0\). The energy argument of Section 3, premise 6, excludes light as the upkeep of a steady pull: a source that kept up its pull by sending out light would send energy out without end, and the count inside any surface around it would fall, and with it the pull, against premise 1. A single change carried by light at a finite speed would cost the source one finite pulse, \(\Delta\mathcal M_{\mathrm{enc}}=-\int P_{\mathrm{out}}\,dT/c^2\), with \(P_{\mathrm{out}}\) the power it sends out. For a change the sum of the pulls decides wherever the pulls on one side come from a body that does not change: when one of two bodies, at the distance \(d\), comes apart with no light into halves at \(\pm s\) across the line, the pulls on the halves come from the body that did not change, at their present places, and add up to \(-Kq\), with \(K=Gm_1m_2/d^2\) for the counts \(m_1\) of that body and \(m_2\) of the other, and \(q=(1+s^2/d^2)^{-3/2}\); that body is pulled by \(+Kq\) at the same moment, and a pull that waited for light would change the momentum of the bodies at the rate \(K(1-q)\), with nothing to hold the difference (the article The Pull That Does Not Wait).

Premise 6 says what is seen: the change is at once, and what an instrument registers comes with the light, so a delay that is observed is the light's (Section 6). The mathematics of what a given instrument registers is still to be written in the paper. The burden is on general relativity's premise that its equations go on holding while the arrangement changes.

Where the results part at high redshift: the observations

The heavier and smaller a body is, the more the light that leaves its surface is redshifted. Take one round body at rest, with the count \(\mathcal M\) as read far away and the radius \(R\), and write \(x=2G\mathcal M/(Rc^2)=L/R\). Two calculations of the redshift \(z\) of the light from its surface (Section 17):

  • The mass held static, without the correction. Every frame held at rest puts the same mass, the far one, into its own law of the pull: \(1+z=1/(1-x/2)\). That breaks premise 1 at once: with \(g_{\mathrm{hold}}=G\mathcal M/r^2\) the clock rate is \(f=1-L/2r\), and the pull counted through the sphere of radius \(r\), \(4\pi r^2f\,g_{\mathrm{hold}}=4\pi G\mathcal M\,(1-L/2r)\), changes from sphere to sphere.

Far from the mass the two agree. Three places, three premises

So the two say the same thing, differently, around one body at rest, outside \(L\) (section 4 above). In three places they say different things, by exact amounts, and in each the burden is on a premise of general relativity:

  • For several bodies, \(q_1q_2\) in the squared clock rate for the same two deficits; inside a body, a different clock: for a count spread evenly in A's units per unit of grid volume, slower at every place where both are real. The premise: the class of equations that Einstein chose (A9), and his equations inside matter, with one mass inside each sphere for every frame.
  • When the count moves, the sum at every moment against a wave with the speed \(c\). The premise: that his equations go on holding while the arrangement changes (A4, A9, A10).
  • At \(L\) and beyond, premises 7 and 8 against one metric continued for every observer. The premise: that continuation, the step that merges the frames' different values into one event horizon, crossed one way only.
