A Relay Race to the Edge

How a line of observers hands one photon along, and why, on A's own clock, it reaches the edge only at infinity.

An article of the section Relativity of Physics: Particle Reconstruction and General Relativity, written for teachers and curious readers. It comes from the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy.

A flashlight, a stopwatch and a very long wait

Here is a puzzle to set a class. An observer we will call A stands on a platform deep inside a black hole, holding a flashlight and a stopwatch. A points the flashlight outward, clicks it on and starts the stopwatch. When the flash reaches the causal boundary, which is also the event horizon, what does the stopwatch read?

The answer, worked out in Section 14 of the paper, is that no finite reading will do. Not a large number: no number at all. On A's clock, the photon reaches the boundary at \(t=\infty\).

And yet nothing happens to the photon there. Every observer it passes on the way reads it going by at the speed of light, and, in the paper's words, "Nothing in the account stops anything at the edge."

Both statements are true. To see how, stop asking A alone. Ask a whole line of observers, each with instruments of their own, passing the photon along like the baton in a relay race. That is the method of this article: use many frames of reference, and the clocks, rulers and scales that relate them, to work out what happens beyond the causal boundary of one of them.

(Standing still inside a black hole? General relativity says nobody can. The paper keeps such frames, and that difference is the heart of the matter. More at the end.)

Everyone is right about their own instruments

Every observer in the story carries three instruments: a clock, a ruler, and a scale that reads energy. Physicists call an observer with their instruments a frame of reference. Premise 7 of the paper (Section 3) begins:

"When two frames of reference observe different values, both are real from the frame of reference that they're observed from."

So nobody is wrong, which is not a bad rule for a staff meeting either. Two observers who read different values for the same photon are both reading their own instruments correctly. The physics is in converting one reading into the other.

The paper converts with one number per place: the clock rate \(f\) of a frame held at rest there, as a share of the clock of an observer at rest far away. The conservation laws fix its square (Section 4, statement 6):

\[ f^2=1-\frac{L}{r}. \]

Here \(r\) is the radius of the circle the frame stands on, and \(L\) is the radius at which a frame at rest would need the speed of light to escape. Far away, \(f=1\). At \(L\), \(f=0\).

Outside \(L\), that one number sets all three instruments of a frame held at rest (premise 5): its clock runs at \(f\) of the far clock, its ruler along the radius is shortened by \(f\), and its scale reads the source larger by \(1/f\). The same number converts readings from frame to frame. A reading \(E_Q\) made by a frame Q becomes, in a frame P (premise 7),

\[ E_P=\frac{f_Q}{f_P}\,E_Q . \]

Think of a currency exchange. A euro is real money in Paris, a yen is real money in Tokyo, and the exchange rate turns one into the other. Here the exchange rate between two places is the ratio of their clock rates.

The relay, leg by leg

The thought experiment of Section 14 puts four places on one line. Take \(L=1{,}000\), in any unit of length you like.

  • A, held at rest at 200, deep inside. (Section 3 of the paper uses the letter A for the observer far away; Section 14, like this story, puts A at 200.)
  • B, held at rest at 900, still inside.
  • C, at 1,000: the causal boundary, which is also the event horizon, of every frame held at rest, A's included. Nobody can be held at rest exactly here: the pull that a frame held at rest feels has no bound where \(f=0\).
  • D, held at rest at 1,100, outside.

A sends a photon outward with the energy \(E_0\) on A's own scale, and B reads it. Inside \(L\) the formula for \(f^2\) gives negative numbers: \(f_A^2=1-1{,}000/200=-4\) and \(f_B^2=-1/9\). Don't panic. Between two frames on the same side of C, the minus signs cancel:

\[ E_B=\frac{f_A}{f_B}\,E_0=\sqrt{\frac{-4}{-1/9}}\;E_0=6\,E_0 . \]

A real number, as it must be: A and B are on the same side of C, and neither is beyond the other's causal boundary.

Now B does what relay runners do and passes the message on. B sends a fresh photon outward carrying the energy it read, \(6E_0\), and the next runner reads that one. Put that runner closer and closer to C, and here is what it reads on its own scale (Section 14, Part 3):

Every reading is real and finite. The readings grow without bound toward C, and the paper is precise about what grows: "what grows without bound at the edge is each frame's reading of it."

Here is the trick that makes the relay so useful: the middle runners drop out. Each runner's clock rate enters the chain twice, once below the line when it reads the photon and once above the line when it hands it on. So what any runner P on A's side of C reads is \(E_0\,f_A/f_P\), whatever runners stand in between and wherever they stand. It is like changing dollars into euros and the euros into yen: you end up at the dollar-to-yen rate, and the euro cancels out. Follow one photon all the way instead of handing on fresh ones, with no energy added from outside, and every reading comes out the same.

One thing the baton never carries: the clocks and rulers themselves. By premise 6 they are updated at once, and light does not carry the change. The only thing handed along the line is the photon.

A's stopwatch runs out of numbers

Now for time. On the photon's side, one fact settles a great deal: light moves at \(c\) on each frame's own clock and ruler (Section 14, Part 10). A runner who times the photon across a stretch of its own ruler gets the speed of light, every time. Nobody along the line sees the photon slow down.

But A does not hold the runners' clocks. A holds A's. To put the photon's trip on A's clock, A converts each runner's stretch with that runner's instruments, and near C two things pile up:

  • each runner's ruler along the radius is shortened by the size of that runner's clock rate, so the same step of radius reads longer and longer on it;
  • each runner's clock, as a share of A's, falls toward zero, so a second on it is worth more and more of A's seconds.

Put together, each stretch adds to A's clock the runner's ruler step divided by the runner's clock share: \(c\,dT_A=d\ell/h\), with \(d\ell=dr/|f|\) and \(h=|f|/|f_A|\). Section 14, Part 10 adds the stretches up, reading A's time at the photon's place through the synchronized frames held at rest along the way:

\[ c\,T_A(r)=|f_A|\,\big[H(R_A)-H(r)\big],\qquad H(r)=r+L\ln\Big|\frac{r}{L}-1\Big| , \]

where \(R_A=200\) is A's place and \(|f_A|=2\) is the size of A's clock rate. Times are given as \(c\) times the time, so they read in the same units as the radius:

Look down the last rows. Each time the photon gets ten times closer to C, A's stopwatch adds about the same amount again. That is what a logarithm does, and the logarithm in \(H\) has no floor: as \(r\) approaches \(L\), it falls without bound, and A's clock reading grows without bound with it. The photon reaches C, A's causal boundary, at no finite time on A's clock: at \(t=\infty\).

It is Zeno's race run backwards. In Zeno's paradox, infinitely many ever-shorter steps add up to a finite time. Here the steps toward C shrink tenfold each time, but on A's stopwatch each one takes about as long as the last, so the sum never closes. If you have ever watched a progress bar crawl from 99% to 99.9% to 99.99%, you know the feeling.

Two more facts make this airtight. First, it does not hang on the size of the rulers: for every scale of the rulers, the photon reaches C at no finite time on A's clock. Second, A's stopwatch is not broken, and A can prove it with a mirror. Put one at B, and the photon comes back to A after \(c\,\Delta T_A=5{,}517.8\) on A's own clock, a reading that needs no clocks anywhere else. That number does depend on the rulers' scale: the round trip is how A measures it. Every trip on A's side of C gets an ordinary finite time. Only C itself does not.

So C is A's causal boundary, "as \(L\) is the far observer's" (Part 10): the edge of A's observable universe. The far observer gets the same verdict at the same radius. On the ruler and clock of an observer at rest far away, light along the radius moves at \(c(1-L/r)\): \(0.0909\,c\) at 1,100, \(0.0099\,c\) at 1,010, \(0.0010\,c\) at 1,001, and zero as the limit at 1,000. On that clock, too, the passage has no finite time (premise 8).

A zero, not a singularity

Where does the infinity come from? From a zero. The clock rate of a frame held at rest is zero at C. That is a zero, not a singularity. It is why no frame can be held at rest at C, and why a conversion factor to a frame there would be zero or without bound (Part 9). The infinity lives in converting into A's clock. It does not live in the photon, which every frame along the way reads at \(c\).

So the paper does not evaluate events at C with a frame held at rest there: there is none. In its own words: "Rather than evaluating events at a causal boundary where no rest frame is held, physics beyond a frame's causal boundary can be calculated indirectly by stepping through intermediate frames of reference" (Section 1). In the next leg the runners stand one on each side of C, and each reads the photon at \(c\) on its own clock and ruler (Parts 3 and 10).

The photon's side of the story

The author calls this method "kind of like a change of variables approach", and every calculus teacher knows the move. A problem that is hopeless in one variable becomes easy in another, and at the end you convert back. Here the hopeless variable is A's clock, which has no finite value for the passage. The good variables are the frames along the photon's way, updated as it goes. Each of them reads the photon passing at \(c\), and each one's reading is real. Then the clock, the ruler and the scale convert the results back into A's frame.

Premise 8 (Section 3) holds both halves of the story, one sentence each:

"Objects can (and do) move beyond their event horizon (also defined as their causal boundary)."

"An observer CANNOT and DOES NOT observe anything passing through the causal boundary."

The photon passes. A never observes it passing. These do not clash: they are two frames' readings, and both are real.

The photon passes through the radius 1,000, and the author has a picture for why no edge stops it there: "It's like the end of a rainbow. It keeps moving and you never reach it." Behind the picture is premise 8: the causal boundary depends on the frame of reference, and the frame of reference changes during motion. The paper derives it in the speed a frame needs to escape: at the radius 1,000, only a frame held at rest needs the speed of light; a frame in free motion needs less: one passing at half the speed of light on its own clock still needs \(0.6\,c\), and one passing at the speed of light needs nothing more (Part 5).

Where general relativity parts company

General relativity tells this story differently, and the difference is a premise, not a calculation. It carries the static form of its solution inside the event horizon by a change of coordinates, and from that one step, with the direction of time it chooses inside, three things follow (Section 18): no frame can be held at rest inside; everything inside falls to the centre; and every frame gets one and the same event horizon, crossed one way only.

So in general relativity's account there is no A at 200 with a stopwatch, no B at 900 to read the photon at \(6E_0\), and no photon moving outward from 200 to 900. This paper keeps those frames, each with real readings (premise 7), and the causal boundary depends on the frame of reference (premise 8). The burden is on general relativity's premise: the step by which it merges the frames' different values into one event horizon.

Next leg: across the line

So far the relay has stayed on A's side of C, where every conversion is an ordinary real number and A's stopwatch handles every trip but one. The next leg crosses the line: one runner just inside C, one just outside, then on to D at 1,100, with A working out the reading of a frame beyond A's own causal boundary. The rule is the same, and the middle runners still drop out. Premise 7 has a second sentence about exactly this kind of working-out, and what the numbers do over there is the subject of the companion article, "What Comes After Forever?"

The premise this contradicts: general relativity's step that merges the frames' different values into one event horizon, crossed one way only, with no frame held at rest inside. The observations that could decide: anything, light or matter, arriving outside from inside an event horizon; or a frame held at rest inside an event horizon, or any signal from one.
