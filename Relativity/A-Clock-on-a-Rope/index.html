A Clock on a Rope

Lower a laboratory toward a black hole, and its clock, its ruler and its scale all change. Cut the rope, and it keeps the ones it had.

An article of the section Relativity of Physics: Particle Reconstruction and General Relativity, written for teachers and curious readers. It comes from the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy.

Heavy for whom?

Ask a class how heavy a black hole is, and someone will offer a number. Ask "heavy for whom?" and the room goes quiet.

The paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy has an exact answer, and all it takes to see it is a very long rope. (We will not ask what the rope is made of.)

Far from a black hole sits observer A. A is the reference: every clock, ruler and unit of energy in this story is compared with A's. To the end of the rope A ties a small laboratory with a good clock, a metre stick, a scale and an accelerometer. Then A lowers the lab toward the black hole, one step at a time, stopping at each step to take readings.

Three things change on the way down, all by one factor that the conservation laws fix. The lab's clock slows. Its ruler along the rope gets shorter. And the black hole, weighed from the lab, comes out heavier. Then the twist: cut the rope, and the lab keeps the clock, the ruler and the scale it had at that moment, all the way down.

Two kinds of frame

The paper sorts frames of reference into two kinds, and an accelerometer at rest in the frame tells them apart (Section 3; Section 4, statement 4).

A frame that needs no force falls freely, and its accelerometer reads zero. Premise 4, in the author's words: "For any frames that don't require an observer at rest in the frame to move to, then there is no redshift and we can keep the same clocks, rulers and mass."

A frame that needs a force has to be held, on a platform or, here, on a rope, and its accelerometer reads the force per unit of mass. Premise 5: "If a force is required, the redshift/clocks/rulers/mass can be calculated using conservation laws."

The paper's version for a child says it best. Premise 4: "If you can get from one place to another without anyone pushing you or holding you, your clock, your ruler and your scale stay the same. Falling freely is like that." Premise 5: "If someone has to push you or hold you, your clock, your ruler and your scale change. The rule that the total never changes tells us by how much."

Our lab on the rope is the second kind. The rope is the quiet hero of this story.

The rule underneath: count everything once

First the count (Section 2). Draw a closed surface around the black hole and everything near it, and let nothing cross it. Write down every kind of energy inside (rest energy, energy of motion, potential energy, heat, light), each one once and all in one frame's units: A's. The total never changes, and neither does its mass equivalent, \(\mathcal M_{\rm enc}=E_{\rm enc}/c^2\). That total sets the pull through every surface outside (premise 1).

Now drop a stone past the lab. Special relativity says the stone speeds up, and the frames it passes read its energy as \(\gamma m_0c^2\), with \(\gamma\) growing all the way down (premise 2). And still the total does not grow. Premise 3, in the paper's version for a teacher: "A fixed total and a growing reading hold together in one way only. The clock of the frame the body passes runs slower by exactly the factor by which the reading has grown: \(\gamma f\) stays the same. That slowing of clocks from place to place is the curvature, and it is the potential energy."

Here \(f\) is the clock rate of the frame held at rest at a place, as a share of A's; it converts that frame's readings into A's units. The work of the pull fixes it. Over each small step \(d\boldsymbol\ell\) the pull \(\mathbf g\), per unit of mass, does work on the stone's mass as it is at that moment, gained energy included, and the clock rates of the frames held at two places 1 and 2 come out in the inverse ratio of the stone's masses there (premise 5):

\[ \frac{f_1}{f_2}=\frac{m_2}{m_1}=\exp\!\left(\int_1^2\frac{\mathbf g\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell}{c^2}\right). \]

The paper calls its whole derivation one idea: "the curvature between two points is the integral of f dot dr" (in that phrase f is the force; everywhere else it is the clock rate). It takes three steps: the work of the pull, the mass of the energy gained, and the clock rate, which falls by the same ratio. "Where a frame has to be held," says the teacher's version of premise 5, "the work of the force gives everything": the clocks and the redshift, the same factor for the ruler along the force, and \(\mathcal M_{\rm enc}/f\) for the mass the frame reads.

Why must the frame change at all? Because the alternative makes energy out of nothing. Keep one frame's scale for every frame, the paper shows, and the entry of a free body changes between two frames "with nothing to pay for it, which is uncompensated kinetic energy". The spontaneous-energy machine of Section 1 is "this loophole in one cycle" (Section 9).

Down the rope

Now lower the lab. Call \(L\) the radius where a frame at rest would need the speed of light to escape: the causal boundary, which is also the event horizon, of a frame held at rest. With \(L=1{,}000\) in any unit you like, as in the paper's worked examples, and with \(r\) the radius of the lab's circle around the black hole (its circumference over \(2\pi\)), this is what the lab reads at each stop (Section 4):

One factor runs the whole table. The clock and the ruler along the rope go as \(f\); the black hole's mass, read on the lab's scale, goes as \(1/f\). Now the three changes in turn.

1. How the black hole's mass changes, and how it doesn't

Weigh the black hole from the lab, and the number comes out bigger the lower you go: 1.414 times A's value at 2 L, 31.6 times at \(r=1{,}001\). Did the black hole gain anything? No. Nothing crossed the surface, so the count is exactly what it was (premise 1). What changed is the lab's unit.

An energy that the held frame reads as \(E\) counts as \(fE\) in A's units. So the held frame's unit of energy is \(f\) times A's, and the count that A reads as \(\mathcal M_{\rm enc}\) reads, in the held frame's unit (Section 4, statement 2),

\[ \mathcal M_{\rm held}=\frac{\mathcal M_{\rm enc}}{f}. \]

A picture: A counts in dollars, and the lab at 2 L counts in a coin worth 0.707 of a dollar. The same treasure comes to 1.414 times as many coins. Nobody added a cent.

This is not a trick of conversion. The lab gets the same number when it weighs the black hole with its own instruments: "its own pull, times the square of the radius of its own circle around the body, over \(G\)". The paper lists the weighing and the count side by side: 1.414214 \(\mathcal M\) both ways at 2 L, 1.732051 \(\mathcal M\) both ways at 1.5 L. And the pull the lab's accelerometer feels, \(G\mathcal M_{\rm enc}/(r^2f)\), is "Newton's law with the mass that it reads itself" (Section 9).

The heavier reading shows in starlight. Hold the mass static instead, giving every held frame the far mass in its own law of the pull, and the light from the surface of a neutron star of 1.4 suns and 12 km comes out redshifted by 20.8 percent, in place of 23.5 (Section 17).

The falling frame needs no force, so it keeps the mass and the unit of the frame it left (premise 4). Released far away, its unit stays A's all the way down.

2. The density of space

The second change is the density of space. A picture of it: near the black hole, space is packed denser along the rope, so the same stretch holds more of the lab's rulers. The physics behind the picture is the paper's ruler rule (Section 4, statement 4).

At any stop, picture a second lab that falls from rest far away and passes ours. How fast is it going? The paper's version for a child: "Exactly as fast as if someone had pushed them, in empty space, with the same push over the same distance that the pull gave them on the way down." Equate the two: from far away the pull raises the falling lab's mass by the factor \(1/f\) (the equation for \(f_1/f_2\) above), and the push raises it by \(\gamma\), so the falling lab passes ours at \(\gamma=1/f\), at Newton's speed of fall.

The falling lab needs no force, so it still has A's clock and A's rulers. Between two frames that move past each other, special relativity changes the clock and the ruler along the motion and leaves the sideways rulers alone. So our lab has its clock at \(f\) of A's, its ruler along the rope at \(f\) of A's, and its sideways rulers the same as A's. A step that A's ruler reads as \(dr\) reads, on the lab's own ruler,

\[ d\ell=\frac{dr}{f}. \]

At 2 L the lab's ruler along the rope is 0.707107 of A's, so a step that A calls one unit, the lab measures as 1.414214. At \(r=1{,}001\) it is 31.6. That is the density: more of the lab's rulers in the same step. Across the rope, nothing changes.

The falling labs' own space has none of it. Frames that fall freely from rest far away have rulers that are exactly A's, in every direction, and "their space is flat everywhere outside": the paper's table gives 1.000000 for their rulers and 0 for the curvature of their space at 1.5 L, 2 L, 5 L and 50 L.

3. The change in clock ticks

"A clock counts the ticks of a transition, and the energy of the transition's light is Planck's constant times its frequency" (Section 4). The lab reads its own transition at the same energy that A reads for A's. Converted to A's units it is \(f\) times that. With the same Planck's constant in every frame, the lab's frequency in A's terms is \(f\) times A's. So the lab's clock runs at \(f\) of A's.

How big is \(f\) at each stop? The count sets the pull through every sphere around the black hole (premise 1). Counted in A's units, the pull on the lab is \(f\,g_{\rm hold}\), where \(g_{\rm hold}\) is the acceleration that holds it at rest, the reading on its accelerometer. That counted pull is Newton's law, exactly, at every strength: \(4\pi r^2f\,g_{\rm hold}=4\pi G\mathcal M_{\rm enc}\). With the clock step of premise 3, \(g_{\rm hold}=c^2\,d\ln f/d\ell\), and the ruler of the last section, the integral of f dot dr gives (statement 6)

\[ f^2=1-\frac{L}{r},\qquad L=\frac{2G\mathcal M_{\rm enc}}{c^2}. \]

Far from the black hole this is \(f\approx1-G\mathcal M/(c^2r)\): "the familiar gravitational redshift". At 2 L, \(f\) is 0.707107; at \(r=1{,}100\), 0.30151; at \(r=1{,}001\), 0.0316. At \(L\), \(f\) is zero, and "a zero of the clock rate is not a singularity" (Section 9).

A sees the slow clock from far away, with the light. In an arrangement at rest every crest takes the same time to climb from the lab to A, so A receives the crests at the rate at which they were sent, counted in A's time: \(1+z=1/f\). The redshift is the lab's clock, read on A's.

There is a second road to the same number. The falling lab, reading our clock with its own rule for "at the same time", finds it running at \(1/\gamma=f\): "the same number as the three steps give, reached by another route."

Cut the rope

Now the contrast. Stop the lab at 2 L and cut the rope. From that moment it is a frame that needs no force: its accelerometer reads zero, and in its own frame no work is done on it (premise 4). It keeps the clock, the rulers and the unit of the frame it left. Its clock, its ruler along the fall and its unit stay at "0.707107 all the way down for a release at 2 L", and its sideways rulers stay A's (Section 4). Between its own frames at any two moments of the fall there is no redshift: \(z=0\).

The frames still hanging on ropes tell the other half. The paper works this very fall through, with \(L=1{,}000\) and the release at 2,000 (Section 4, statement 6):

The held frames read the lab's energy growing. Their own clocks run slower by exactly the same factor. The product, which the paper calls the body's count \(e=\gamma f\), never moves: "At every step the energy gained equals the drop in the clock rate: the curvature cancels the gain exactly." Near \(L\) the reading grows without bound while the count stays fixed. The child's version: "The blow-up is in the reading, not in what is there."

So a frame of reference changes in one of two ways. Held on the rope, the lab's own clock, ruler and scale change at every stop, by the f dot dr of the force that holds it. Falling free, the lab keeps its own, and what changes along the way is its reading in the frames it passes, \(\gamma=e/f\). Premise 8: "The frame of reference changes during motion." And: "When the frame of reference changes, it's possible that items will exist, or no longer exist, in the frame of reference of the observer. This isn't because things pass through the causal boundary of a frame of reference. It's because a particle moved, and now has a new frame of reference." And every change is at once. Premise 6, for a child: "When something moves, every clock and every ruler changes at once. Light does not carry the change to them."

At the bottom of the rope

Section 4's tables go as deep as \(r=1{,}001\), where the lab's clock runs at 0.0316 of A's and the lab reads the black hole at 31.6 times A's value. No rope holds the lab at \(L\) itself: no frame is held at rest there, because a frame held at rest feels the pull \(G\mathcal M_{\rm enc}/(r^2f)\), which has no bound where \(f=0\) (Section 14).

The causal boundary depends on the frame of reference (premise 8). At \(L\) a frame at rest would need the speed of light to escape. A frame in free motion with count \(e\le1\) needs \(c(1-e^2)/(1+e^2)\) along its motion, \(0.6\,c\) for \(e=0.5\), and with \(e\ge1\) it needs nothing at all. The author's picture: "It's like the end of a rainbow. It keeps moving and you never reach it."

"Nothing ends at \(L\)" (Section 14). Our cut lab reaches it after a finite time on its own clock, still moving at \(c\,e\), and moves beyond it. Inside, on its own clock, the pull points outward: the lab turns at \(L/(1+e^2)\) and comes back out through \(L\) with its count \(e\) (premise 8). A, far away, cannot and does not observe the passage; on A's clock it has no finite time. The paper keeps frames held at rest inside \(L\) as well, each with readings that are real for it (premise 7): a story for another article.

One frame's numbers for everyone

Around one body at rest, outside \(L\), general relativity's solution has these same clocks and rulers; the paper derives them from the count, with no equation of Einstein's (Sections 4 and 9). The two part at \(L\). General relativity carries the outside form inside by a change of coordinates, and with that step "gives every frame one mass \(\mathcal M\) and one event horizon, leaves no frame at rest inside" (Section 18), and nothing crosses that horizon outward. The paper's version for a teacher puts the habit in one line: "Both derivations take one frame's numbers for everyone" (Section 1).

The lab on the rope says otherwise. Each frame held at rest has its own clock, its own ruler and its own reading of the mass, all fixed by the conservation laws, and "when two frames of reference observe different values, both are real from the frame of reference that they're observed from" (premise 7). Merging them into one mass and one event horizon for every frame is the step the paper names as the error. None of it is needed outside the horizon, and the burden is on that step (Section 18).

The premise of general relativity this article contradicts: one mass and one event horizon for every frame of reference, given to all of them by carrying the outside form across \(L\), and crossed one way only. The observation that could decide: anything, light or matter, arriving outside from inside an event horizon.
