The Pull That Does Not Wait

A rock comes apart without a flash, and the conservation laws show why every pull, clock and ruler is updated at once, with no light to carry the change.

An article of the section Relativity of Physics: Particle Reconstruction and General Relativity, written for teachers and curious readers. It comes from the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy.

A riddle for the whiteboard

Here is a riddle for your next class. Two rocks float in deep space, far from everything else. One of them quietly comes apart into two halves: no flash, no bang, no light of any kind. How long is it before the pull on the other rock changes?

The textbook answer: as long as light would take to cross the gap. In general relativity a change of clocks and rulers moves out as a front at the speed of light and carries energy and momentum, and the far rock is pulled as before until light could have crossed.

The paper "Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy" answers: no time at all. Its premise 6 reads:

"The rulers are updated instantaneously (not transferred via information carried by light and at the speed of light). A clock and a ruler made of light do NOT carry the result to every clock and ruler. Requiring this would violate conservation of mass-energy."

In short: if the information had to be carried by light, the conservation laws would break. That can sound like heresy, so let's test it with two rocks, a spring, and the two conservation laws every student learns.

Two rules and a jar

The paper builds on two rules (Section 2). Draw a closed surface around a group of bodies, a jar with the lid on, and let nothing cross it. The complete energy inside, counted once, does not change. The total momentum inside does not change either: in the paper's words for children, "every push is matched by an equal push the other way."

Premise 6 adds the sentence that does the work in this article: "The bodies hold all the momentum, and clocks and rulers hold none." So the total momentum is a sum over the bodies alone, and it never changes:

\[ \mathbf P=\sum_i\mathbf p_i,\qquad \frac{d\mathbf P}{dT}=\sum_i\mathbf F_i(T)=0 . \]

Read the second equation slowly. Add up every pull on every body, all at the same moment \(T\), and you must get zero. Get anything else, and the momentum of the bodies changes by itself, with nothing else in the jar to hold the difference.

"The same moment" has a precise meaning: "\(T\) is the time that the frames with no force share", the time shared by observers who float freely and feel no force.

The quiet split

Now the story. A big rock, call her Bertha, floats at rest. At a distance \(d\) floats a smaller rock, the Twins: two equal halves held together by a latch, with a squeezed spring between them. Bertha and the Twins pull on each other with Newton's pull, \(K=Gm_1m_2/d^2\), where \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) are the masses of Bertha and the Twins.

Click. The latch lets go, and the spring pushes the halves apart sideways, one up and one down, to a distance \(s\) on either side of where the Twins sat. The spring pushes the two halves equally and oppositely, so its pushes cancel. No light is sent out. In space, no one can hear you split.

Freeze the moment \(T\) when the halves sit at their new places, and fill in the ledger.

The halves first. Bertha has not changed at all, so her pull on each half is her pull at the place where that half is now. Each half is a little farther away than the Twins were, and each is pulled partly sideways. The sideways parts cancel, one up and one down. What is left, along the line to Bertha, is a share \(q\) of the old pull:

\[ \text{pull on the halves}=-Kq,\qquad q=\Big(1+\frac{s^2}{d^2}\Big)^{-3/2}. \]

The minus sign means toward Bertha. The number \(q\) is less than one, and the farther apart the halves, the smaller it is.

Now Bertha. The pulls must add to zero at this same moment, so Bertha must feel \(+Kq\), at once. And \(+Kq\) is exactly the pull of the two halves where they are now. Nothing is transferred from the Twins to Bertha. Momentum conservation has fixed her pull to the halves' present places, at the very moment they are there.

That is what makes the split so clean. The pulls on the halves come from Bertha, who has not changed, so the only entry left open in the ledger is Bertha's own pull, and momentum conservation fills it in.

What if the pull waited for light?

Suppose instead that Bertha's pull had to wait for light. Until light could cross the gap to her, she would still be pulled as before, by \(+K\), toward where the Twins used to be. Add up the ledger:

\[ K-Kq=K(1-q)\neq0 . \]

The pulls no longer add to zero. The total momentum of the rocks would change at the rate \(K(1-q)\), with nothing outside the jar pushing on them.

Where could the missing momentum hide? Not in the rocks: all three pieces are counted. Not in light: none was sent out. Not in the clocks and rulers: they hold none. There is nowhere left.

A halfway answer fails as well. The companion document "A Force-Carrying Geometric Excitation" puts it in one line: "Every departure from \(+Kq\), including a partial or late response, leaves momentum unbalanced by the amount of the departure." A pull that is partly updated fails as surely as one that waits.

This evaluation of the pulls is exact at the places where the halves sit, and as the pieces go on moving freely it holds to the first order in the pulls. The momentum balance itself holds exactly, at every moment.

Every probe and every clock

Bertha is not special. Put a tiny still probe anywhere that Newton's far-away pull holds, responding to the pull at its own place as the halves do, and the same ledger fixes its pull at once, at the value the present places give. The paper's clock step turns pulls into clock rates from place to place:

\[ d\ln f=\frac{g\,d\ell}{c^2}, \]

where \(f\) is the clock rate of a frame held at rest, as a share of a clock far away (where \(f=1\)), \(g\) the acceleration that holds it there and \(d\ell\) a step on its own ruler. Apply the step at each moment \(T\), and the rates of the clocks held at rest follow at once. The probe's pull and these clock rates follow to the same first order in the pulls.

The paper's premises carry the rest. Premise 5 calculates a held frame's clock, ruler and scale (the mass it reads) from the work of the force that holds it. Section 4, statement 8, gives the clocks at every place "with each body's place and count at that moment and the ruler along the force of that moment." (A count is a complete energy over \(c^2\).) And the paper's version of premise 6 for children says it in three sentences: "When something moves, every clock and every ruler changes at once. Light does not carry the change to them. If light had to carry it, the total would not stay the same."

Why light cannot be the messenger

Premise 6 opens with another argument, which needs no split at all. Take one rock, sitting perfectly still. Its pull is there at every distance, at every moment. If light had to carry the result to every clock and ruler, the rock would be a lighthouse that can never switch off: in the paper's words, "the source would send energy out without end."

Light carries energy. Every flash that leaves the jar lowers the count inside, and the pull falls with it:

\[ \frac{d\mathcal M_{\rm enc}}{dT}\lt0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{d\Phi_g}{dT}\lt0 . \]

Here \(\mathcal M_{\rm enc}\) is the count inside a closed surface around the rock, its complete energy over \(c^2\), and \(\Phi_g\) is the pull through that surface. Premise 1 gives a still rock, with nothing crossing its surface, a pull that never changes. A rock that kept up its pull by sending out light would be a rock whose pull wears away. In the companion document's words, "continual net outward light cannot maintain the unchanged pull of an unpowered steady source."

A merry-go-round that pushes itself

The split looks at one moment. For bodies that keep moving, the companion document tries a second arrangement. Two equal bodies sit at opposite ends of a diameter of a circle of radius \(a\), each moving at speed \(v\). Now give them a pull that waits for light: each is pulled, with strength \(Q\), toward where the other was one light-crossing time ago, the time light needs to get from one to the other.

While that light was on its way, the partner moved on round the circle. So the line to its old place leans away from the line to where it is now, by an angle \(\delta\), and it leans forward, in the direction the body is going. Part of each pull, \(Q\sin\delta\), now pushes its body along its path:

\[ \dot E=2vQ\sin\delta,\qquad \delta=\frac vc\cos\delta . \]

In words: the pair gains energy at the rate \(2vQ\sin\delta\): two bodies, each pushed forward by \(Q\sin\delta\) while it moves at \(v\). It gains angular momentum as well, at the rate \(2aQ\sin\delta\). The second equation sets the lean: the faster the bodies move compared with light, the bigger \(\delta\).

Energy and angular momentum from nothing: a merry-go-round that pushes itself. The paper's Introduction warns of such a machine. In its words for children, if "someone forgets to update the ruler as things move", "you could build a trick machine inside the bubble that makes extra energy out of nothing." With no other entry in the ledger to pay for the gain, conservation rules out a pull aimed at old places for the circling pair.

No memory allowed

The circle is one arrangement. The companion document also derives a rule for any number of bodies that move. Write \(q_0\) for where all the bodies are now, and \(q_1\) to \(q_m\) for where they were at finitely many earlier moments, at distinct positive lags. The rule, as written there:

"If the complete energy, apart from constant rest energy, is \(\sum_iK_i+U(q_0,q_1,\ldots,q_m)\), with \(U\) differentiable and counted once, and the sampled position rates can vary independently at fixed sampled positions, the work law and energy conservation require the potential to depend only on the present configuration, a pull that depends only on those positions to be its negative gradient, and any velocity-dependent remainder that is finite at zero present velocity to have zero summed power."

Here \(K_i\) is the energy of motion of body \(i\) (these are new letters, not the split's \(K\) and \(q\)), and \(U\) is a stored energy that is allowed, at the start, to remember earlier places. Conservation takes that memory away. A pull set by positions alone is the downhill slope of an energy of the present places, and any extra part that depends on the velocities does no net work on the bodies taken together. Beyond these conditions, and for the rulers around a count that moves, the rule is premise 6 itself.

What changes at once

Put together, the picture is simple. At every moment \(T\), the pull through every closed surface is set by the count inside it at that moment, "with no term delayed by the time light takes":

\[ \Phi_g(T)=4\pi G\,\mathcal M_{\rm enc}(T). \]

Premise 6, in its version for teachers: "The pull on a body at any moment is the pull toward where the other bodies are at that moment, and momentum is conserved." Around the whole group, "the pull of the whole is that of one point mass at every moment, whatever the bodies inside do" (Section 4, statement 8). The split leaves the count of Bertha and the Twins together unchanged, so the pull through a surface around all three pieces does not change at all.

Light keeps its own job. It moves at the speed of light, it carries energy, and what an instrument registers comes with it, so a delay that is observed is the light's (Section 6). What does not wait is the pull, and with it the clocks, the rulers and the scales.

Where general relativity parts ways

General relativity's premise at this step is that a change of clocks and rulers moves out as a front at the speed of light and carries energy and momentum. In Einstein's derivation, laid out in Section 5 of the paper, his equations go on holding while the arrangement changes; to first order they give a wave equation for a change of the clocks and rulers, and such a wave moves at the speed of light. General relativity reads that wave as carrying energy and momentum away.

In general relativity's account of the split, that wave is the only place left where the missing momentum could be held, and the burden is on that premise. The paper notes that "Section 5 itself gives the pattern of clocks and rulers no local density of energy (part 12)." In the paper's account, the bodies hold all the momentum, and clocks and rulers hold none. Section 4, statement 10, sets the two side by side: "When the count moves, premise 6 holds: the rulers are updated instantaneously, and the sum of statement 8 holds at every moment. In Einstein's derivation a change moves at the speed of light."

The split names the observation that could decide. Watch the still body: does it move in answer to the split before light could cross the gap? Premise 6 says it does, at once. Section 6 gives the general form: "whether an instrument takes up energy, or recoils, when a count far from it moves, with no light from the event; and when an instrument registers the change, compared with the light from the same event".
