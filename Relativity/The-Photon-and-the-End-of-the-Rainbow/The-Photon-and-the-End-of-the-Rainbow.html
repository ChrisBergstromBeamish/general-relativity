The Photon and the End of the Rainbow

How light gets past a black hole's event horizon, told from the frame of an observer who is not standing still.

An article of the section Relativity of Physics: Particle Reconstruction and General Relativity, written for teachers and curious readers. It comes from the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy.

"Nothing can escape a black hole, not even light." It may be the most quoted line in modern physics. It has sold a great many books, and it is a wonderful thing to say to a class just before the bell.

The paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy shows that the line is wrong. Its premise 8, in the author's words: "Objects can (and do) move beyond their event horizon (also defined as their causal boundary)."

This article follows one photon out. The author's answer to why it gets out is short: the photon escapes because its frame of reference is continually updated, and the event horizon, the causal boundary, changes during the journey outward. Here is what that means, in the paper's own numbers.

The edge belongs to a frame

Start with the paper's definition (premise 8): "A frame's event horizon is where that frame would need the speed of light to escape."

Notice the words that frame. A frame of reference is an observer with a clock, a ruler and a scale, and each frame has its own event horizon. It is the same thing as that frame's causal boundary: the edge of what the frame can observe, the edge of its observable universe.

Outside the black hole, a frame held at rest at radius \(r\) needs the escape velocity \(\sqrt{2GM/r}\). That reaches \(c\) at \(L=2GM/c^2\), and the paper's thought experiment sets \(L=1{,}000\). (A radius here is the radius of the circles around the black hole: a circle's circumference divided by \(2\pi\). Times are given as the distance light covers in them, in the same units.) For a frame held at rest, \(L\) is the radius of the causal boundary, which is also the event horizon.

At \(L\) the clock of a frame held at rest would run at the rate zero. That is a zero, not a singularity, and nothing ends at \(L\). It does mean that no frame can be held at rest exactly there: the pull such a frame would feel has no bound.

Now the key fact. The speed a frame still needs at \(L\) to escape depends on how the frame moves:

\[ \text{held at rest:}\ \ c,\qquad\qquad \text{in free motion with count } e:\ \ c\,\frac{1-e^2}{1+e^2}. \]

With a count of 1 or more, a frame in free motion needs nothing at all. The count \(e\) comes in a moment; first, the paper's table (Section 14):

Only a frame held at rest needs the full speed of light, and only at \(L\). A frame in free motion needs less than \(c\) at every radius it reaches. As the paper's version for a child puts it: "The edge is not the same for everyone: it depends on who is looking, and it changes as you move."

Meet the observer at the base

The author puts the observer of this story "at the base of the black hole", and describes her frame like this: "The observer isn't at rest, but the frame of reference is useful because it's ruler/scale/mass are constant."

Call her Iris, after the goddess of the rainbow. You will see why.

The paper calls her frame a frame in free motion: a frame that needs no force. An accelerometer at rest in it reads zero. Premise 4, in the author's words, says what such a frame buys you: "For any frames that don't require an observer at rest in the frame to move to, then there is no redshift and we can keep the same clocks, rulers and mass."

The paper's version for a child says it best: "If you can get from one place to another without anyone pushing you or holding you, your clock, your ruler and your scale stay the same." No force is needed, so no work is done on Iris, and between her frames at any two moments there is no redshift. She is the lab partner every teacher dreams of: her watch, her tape measure and her scale never need recalibrating, however far she goes.

She carries one more thing that never changes: her count, \(e=\gamma f\). Every frame held at rest that she passes reads her energy as \(\gamma\) times her rest energy. Multiply that reading by the frame's clock rate \(f\), as a share of the far observer's clock (inside \(L\), by its real size, \(\sqrt{L/r-1}\)), and you get the same number, \(e\), all along her path. Times her rest energy, it is her entry in the count of the observer far away, and that it never changes while she moves freely is conservation of mass-energy (premise 3). Far from the black hole, that entry is the one your students already know: Newton's conserved energy, with the rest energy added.

Iris has \(e=0.5\). Where is her base? Inside \(L\), the frames held at rest read her at \(\gamma=e/\sqrt{L/r-1}\). At \(r=800\) that is exactly her rest energy. One step deeper, it would be less than her rest energy, which no speed allows. So 800 is the deepest a body with her count ever is. There, for an instant, she comes to rest. Then she goes back out.

Step by step out

Inside \(L\) the pull points outward (Section 14, Parts 6 and 10): a body at rest there cannot fall inward, it can only move out. So from her base Iris heads out, and the journey itself speeds her up. Hold on to that: the energy for her way out is obtained on the way out.

Here is her trip, in the paper's numbers. Her speed is on her own clock: how fast her radius grows, per tick of her own watch.

  1. Her base, 800. Speed zero. To escape she would still need \(0.866\,c\).
  2. 900. Speed \(0.373\,c\). The frame held at rest here reads her at one and a half times her rest energy.
  3. 990. Speed \(0.490\,c\).
  4. \(L\), at 1,000. Speed \(0.500\,c\), which is \(c\) times her count: not zero. She got here after 744.3 on her own clock, a finite time. To escape she would now need only \(0.6\,c\). A frame held at rest here would need \(c\), and no frame can be held here.
  5. Beyond \(L\). Speed \(0.490\,c\) at 1,010 and \(0.399\,c\) at 1,100. Her count is below 1, so she climbs to 1,333.3, where she again needs \(0.866\,c\), and falls back. On her own clock she goes on passing through \(L\), both ways.

At every step something is updated: her place, the frames she passes, their readings of her, and the speed she still needs to escape. "The frame of reference changes during motion" (premise 8). Near \(L\) the readings of the frames held at rest grow without bound. In the paper's words: "Nothing that moves toward the edge loses its entry; what grows without bound at the edge is each frame's reading of it. Nothing in the account stops anything at the edge." Her clock, her ruler, her scale and her count stay exactly as they were.

Now look at what she still needs: \(0.866\,c\) at her base, \(0.6\,c\) at \(L\), and less than \(c\) at every radius she reaches. By the paper's definition, her event horizon is never where she is.

The author has a picture for this: "Yeah, this is the radius of the causal boundary which is also the event horizon. However, an photon leaving will pass thru that location. The reason is one of our premises. During the journey, the causal boundard keep changing. It's like the end of a rainbow. It keeps moving and you never reach it."

The rainbow is a picture (and the reason nobody has ever collected the leprechaun's gold). The mathematics under it is the speed a frame needs to escape, which depends on the frame: \(c\) for a frame held at rest at \(L\), and \(c(1-e^2)/(1+e^2)\) for a frame in free motion, which for Iris is \(0.6\,c\).

The photon leaves

At \(L\), Iris switches on a lamp and sends one photon outward, with energy \(E\) on her own scale.

The paper gives what an observer far away receives from light sent outward at a radius \(r\) outside \(L\) (premise 8; Section 14, Part 8):

\[ fE\ \ \text{(source at rest)},\qquad \big(e-\sqrt{e^2-F}\,\big)E\ \ \text{(falling in)},\qquad \big(e+\sqrt{e^2-F}\,\big)E\ \ \text{(on its way out)},\qquad F=1-\frac{L}{r}. \]

At \(L\) itself, \(F=0\). The first two give zero. The third gives \(2eE\), and for Iris, with \(e=0.5\), that is \(E\) itself: real and finite. Same place, three kinds of motion, three answers. Premise 8 says what this means: "When the frame of reference changes, it's possible that items will exist, or no longer exist, in the frame of reference of the observer. This isn't because things pass through the causal boundary of a frame of reference. It's because a particle moved, and now has a new frame of reference."

The photon also reaches the far observer at a finite time on the far observer's clock. The paper sends it closer and closer to \(L\), one part in a hundred, in ten thousand and in a million outside it, and it reaches 1,100 ahead of Iris by 215.5, 235.6 and 235.8. In the paper's words, "the two far times that grow without bound cancel."

Her own passage is a different story. On the far observer's clock, her trip from those same three points to 1,100 takes 2,608, 7,243 and 11,849, growing without bound. On that clock she has been just outside \(L\) since the earliest times. The far observer never observes Iris pass \(L\), and still receives the photon she sent there.

So why does the photon get out? Because it does not leave from a frame held at rest, or from one falling in. It leaves from Iris's frame, updated at every step of the journey out, and at \(L\) that frame needs only \(0.6\,c\) to escape. By the paper's definition, \(L\) is not her event horizon.

And where does the energy come from? At \(L\) the photon has a count only because its source is on its way out; from a source at rest or falling in, the count would be zero. Iris is on her way out because the journey out, from rest at her base, set her moving. The author puts it plainly: "Our math says that nothing can escape the black hole without using energy obtained during the journey out."

Nobody watches the crossing

Premise 8 says it outright: "An observer CANNOT and DOES NOT observe anything passing through the causal boundary."

The paper shows this deep inside too, with a frame Section 14 calls A, held at rest at 200. A sends a photon outward with energy \(E_0\) on its own scale.

On A's own clock the photon is at 900 after 2,758.9, at 990 after 7,184.1, at 999 after 11,771.2, at 999.9 after 16,374.6 and at 999.99 after 20,979.6. It reaches \(L\) at no finite time on A's clock. \(L\) is A's causal boundary, the edge of A's observable universe.

And yet the photon passes that place. The paper follows it out, and "at each step it carries on the energy that the frame there reads, and no energy is added from outside." The frames held at rest along the way read it at 6 times A's energy at 900, 19.900 times at 990, 63.214 at 999, 199.990 at 999.9 and 632.452 at 999.99. Its energy for the way out is gained on the way out. A frame just outside \(L\) reads it too, and so does a frame at 1,100. Each reads it with its own clock, ruler and scale, and each reading is real for the frame that makes it (premise 7).

Only when A works out a reading across its own causal boundary, through the frames in between, does it get two answers, both complex: for the frame at 1,100, \(\pm6.633\,i\,E_0\). Across \(L\) the square of the factor between two frames' clock rates is negative, because the square of a clock rate is \(1-L/r\), positive outside \(L\) and negative inside; and a negative number has two square roots, \(+i\) and \(-i\) times a real number.

In the author's words: "The photon observes itself passing thu the event horizon of Observer A. Observer A doesn't observe the photon passing thru it's event horizon." The crossing "does happen based on the continously updated frame of reference."

Two last details. Through the readings, the photon's own energy does not move the edge: double \(E_0\) and every reading on every leg doubles, while the place of the edge stays where it is. It counts only as part of what is inside: while the photon is inside, its energy is one of the entries that set \(L\), and when it has left, that entry leaves with it (Parts 1 and 4). And a body let go at rest at A's place, instead of held there, comes out as well, on the energy its journey out gives it: it reaches \(L\) after 529.1 on its own clock, leaves with count 2, needs no extra speed at all, and goes away for good, reaching far away at \(0.866\,c\) (Part 6).

Where general relativity takes a wrong turn

General relativity hands every frame the same event horizon: one \(L\), measured far away, for every frame inside and out, and nothing crosses it outward. That comes from a single step: the Schwarzschild form, derived outside the horizon, is carried inside by a change of coordinates (Section 7, parts 15 to 17). The same step leaves no frame at rest inside and, with the direction of time it chooses there, sends everything inside to the centre.

The paper names the premises this rests on (Section 18): (1) the static form carried inside the horizon; (2) no frame can be held at rest inside it; (3) one event horizon for every frame, crossed one way only. "These three are the error, and the burden is on them."

Iris's photon contradicts premise (3) directly. At \(L\) only a frame held at rest needs the speed of light; Iris needs \(0.6\,c\), and the photon she sends there is received far away with the energy \(E\). The fall to the centre goes too: at her base, where she comes to rest and turns back, general relativity's continued solution still has her moving inward.

Why is this so easy to miss? Section 13 gives three reasons. Outside the horizon the two agree exactly, so the familiar tests of clocks, such as GPS, pass either way. Textbook notation makes the reading of one frame look like the count. And no direct observation has ever probed the inside of a horizon.

What would decide it? The paper names the observation: anything, light or matter, arriving outside from inside an event horizon. General relativity's one-way horizon forbids it; in this paper, objects can, and do, move beyond their event horizon, by the same law in both directions. That one-way horizon has never been tested: no observation has yet been made at or inside an event horizon.
