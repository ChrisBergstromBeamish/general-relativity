Who Pays for the Falling Stone?

General relativity's clocks and rulers in five steps from conservation of energy, and a two-charge machine that shows why every stored energy must be counted, once

An article of the section Relativity of Physics: Particle Reconstruction and General Relativity, written for teachers and curious readers. It comes from the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy.

Here is a puzzle to try on a class.

Seal a heavy body and a stone inside a jar. Nothing gets in, nothing gets out. Let the stone fall. It speeds up, and the energy of its motion has mass, so the stone gets heavier as it falls.

The jar can't. Nothing crossed the lid. Whatever the stone gained, something else in the jar had to give up, exactly.

So who pays?

The answer of the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy is: the clocks. Follow that answer for five short steps and out come the clock rate and the ruler of general relativity's solution outside a round body, with none of Einstein's equations used. Then a machine built from two electric charges, which makes energy out of nothing as long as you count wrong, shows why the jar's count must hold every kind of stored energy, each one once.

The rule of the jar

Draw a closed surface around the heavy body and the stone: the jar. The paper calls it a Gaussian surface, after the surface in Gauss's law. Put an observer, A, on a second surface outside it, far away. Now list every kind of energy inside: rest energy, energy of motion, potential and binding energy, heat, light. Count each once, all in A's units. Divide the total by \(c^2\) and you have the jar's mass, \(\mathcal M_{\rm enc}\).

That count sets the pull through A's surface. With the lid on, the count can't change, so neither can the pull:

\[ \Phi_g=4\pi G\,\mathcal M_{\rm enc},\qquad \mathcal M_{\rm enc}=\frac{E_{\rm enc}}{c^2},\qquad \frac{d\Phi_g}{dt}=0 . \]

This is the paper's first premise, in the author's words: "The gravitational force between two Gaussian surfaces is time invariant." That, the paper says, is conservation of mass-energy (Sections 2 and 3).

The stone that gets heavier

Now let the stone fall past a row of observers held at rest at different heights, each standing on a platform. Each one reads the passing stone's energy as \(\gamma m_0c^2\), with special relativity's \(\gamma\) (premise 2). Lower down the stone is faster, so \(\gamma\) is bigger, all the way down.

A fixed total and a growing reading. The paper's third premise says how both can be true: "The only way for the force to be invariant is for general relativity to curve space. Any potential energy (gravitational potential, electrostatic potential) must curve space so that when the potential energy is released, the curvature changes to nullify the increase in kinetic energy."

"Curve space" here means something you could measure with a stopwatch and a metre stick: the clocks and rulers of the observers held at rest. Five steps show how (Section 4, statements 1 to 6).

Five steps to general relativity's clocks

Step 1: the pull makes the stone heavier

Write \(m=\gamma m_0\) for the stone's mass as a held observer reads it. On each small step \(d\ell\) of the fall, the pull \(g\) does work on the stone. It works on the mass as it is at that moment, the energy already gained included, and the work has a mass of its own, the work over \(c^2\). So on every step the mass grows by a share of itself:

\[ \frac{dm}{m}=\frac{g\,d\ell}{c^2}. \]

Step 2: the clock pays

The jar can't gain, so the stone's gain is canceled right where it happens: the clock of the observer held at that place runs slow by exactly the same share. Call \(f\) that clock's rate, as a share of A's. It is also the exchange rate between the two observers' energies: an energy the held observer reads as \(E\) counts as \(fE\) in A's units. (Planck's relation, with one constant in every frame, ties the unit of energy to the clock.) From place to place the clock rate changes by

\[ d\ln f=\frac{g_{\rm hold}\,d\ell}{c^2}, \]

where \(g_{\rm hold}\) is the acceleration that holds the observer at rest (the pull \(g\) of step 1, reversed) and \(d\ell\) is a step on that observer's own ruler. The reading \(\gamma\) grows by exactly the share by which \(f\) falls, so the product \(\gamma f\), which the paper calls the stone's count \(e\), never changes. Far from the body, where \(f\approx1+\Phi/c^2\) with Newton's potential \(\Phi\), the stone's entry in A's count is \(\gamma f\,m_0c^2\approx m_0c^2+\tfrac12m_0v^2+m_0\Phi\): Newton's conserved energy, plus the rest energy. Near the body it is the same \(f\) and the same rule. The potential energy is the clock rate.

Step 3: a stone dropped from far away

Drop a second stone from rest beside A. It starts with A's clock, so its count is \(\gamma f=1\), and it keeps it. So it passes each held observer at

\[ \gamma=\frac1f,\qquad v=c\sqrt{1-f^2}. \]

The paper reaches the same number a second way: the pull's work on the way down is exactly the work a push would do on a body in empty space over the same stretch, and special relativity turns that work into the same \(\gamma\). Nobody pushes or holds this stone. An accelerometer riding with it reads zero. No force, no work, so it keeps A's clock, A's rulers and A's mass (premise 4).

Step 4: the ruler

As they pass, the held observer and the falling stone are simply two frames moving past each other at \(\gamma\), and special relativity at one place applies: it changes the clock and the ruler along the motion by the factor \(1/\gamma=f\), and it leaves the sideways rulers alone. The falling stone carries A's clock and rulers. So the held observer's clock runs at \(f\) of A's (step 2's result, by another road), the held ruler along the pull is \(f\) of A's, and the sideways rulers match A's. A step \(dr\) on A's ruler reads, on the held observer's shorter ruler,

\[ d\ell=\frac{dr}{f}. \]

Step 5: count the pull through a sphere

Back to the rule of the jar. Draw a sphere around the body, with \(r\) its circumference over \(2\pi\). The sideways rulers did not change, so its area is \(4\pi r^2\). Add up the pull felt by the observers held on it, each counted in A's units, \(f\,g_{\rm hold}\). The count sets the total:

\[ 4\pi r^2\,f\,g_{\rm hold}=4\pi G\,\mathcal M_{\rm enc}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f^2=1-\frac{L}{r},\qquad L=\frac{2G\mathcal M_{\rm enc}}{c^2}. \]

The arrow is one substitution. Step 2 gives \(g_{\rm hold}=c^2\,d\ln f/d\ell\), and step 4 gives \(d\ell=dr/f\). Together, \(f\,g_{\rm hold}=\tfrac12c^2\,d(f^2)/dr\), so the sphere's equation reads \(r^2\,d(f^2)/dr=L\). Set \(f=1\) far away, where clocks agree with A's, integrate once, and \(f^2=1-L/r\) drops out.

On the way, the sphere's equation says something worth a second look: the pull counted in A's units is Newton's \(G\mathcal M_{\rm enc}/r^2\), exactly, at every strength.

Where general relativity comes out

Put the clock and the ruler together, with the sideways rulers unchanged:

\[ ds^2=-\Big(1-\frac Lr\Big)c^2dt^2+\frac{dr^2}{1-L/r}+r^2d\Omega^2 . \]

This is general relativity's solution outside a round body, the Schwarzschild solution. Its clock rate is the \(f\) of step 5, its ruler along the radius is the \(dr/f\) of step 4, its last term \(r^2d\Omega^2\) is the sideways rulers, unchanged, and \(L\) is the radius where the escape velocity of a frame at rest, \(\sqrt{2G\mathcal M_{\rm enc}/r}\), reaches \(c\).

Extras come free. Far away, \(f\approx1-G\mathcal M/(c^2r)\): the familiar gravitational redshift. The stone dropped beside A passes each held observer at \(\sqrt{2G\mathcal M_{\rm enc}/r}\), Newton's speed of fall, and on its own clock it obeys Newton's law exactly, \(d^2r/d\tau^2=-G\mathcal M_{\rm enc}/r^2\).

What went in: the conservation of energy and of momentum; three of the paper's premises, each one load bearing: the pull through every sphere is set by the count (premise 1, step 5), the clock rate is the exchange rate (premise 3, step 2), and the stone dropped from far away keeps A's clock and rulers (premise 4, steps 3 and 4); special relativity at one place; Planck's relation; and Newton's law far away, which fixes \(G\). Leave the ruler of step 4 unchanged, \(d\ell=dr\), and the same count and clock step give \(f=1-L/2r\) instead. What did not: no equation of Einstein's, no one geometry shared by all observers, no general covariance, no condition that the equations be of second order, and no assumption that the arrangement is the same at every time. Things Einstein's derivation assumes come out here as results, among them the equivalence principle (no force, no work, so the same clocks, rulers and mass), Newton's law (exact, once \(G\) is fixed far away) and the mass of the source (the complete count inside a closed surface). In the paper's words: "Outside, the Schwarzschild solution is conservation of mass-energy."

Read across any row: the energy gained equals the drop in the clock rate. The clocks pay, to the last digit.

A picture for the classroom: step 1 is compound interest. The pull works on the mass already gained, so the growth compounds. From 2000 to 1100 the integral of step 1, divided by \(c^2\), is 0.852374, and \(\exp(0.852374)=2.34521\), the mass ratio and the clock ratio both. Simple interest, with the mass held fixed, would give 1.852374, and the count would not close.

Near \(L\) the reading \(\gamma\) grows without bound, while the entry \(\gamma f\) stays at 0.70711. At \(L\) the clock rate of a frame held at rest is zero. That is a zero, not a singularity. As the paper puts it for children: "The blow-up is in the reading, not in what is there."

Agreement is not the result

Around one body at rest, outside \(L\), the conservation derivation and general relativity give the same clocks and the same rulers, to every figure. That agreement is not the result, and agreement there can't tell the two apart. The paper names three places where they part: for several bodies, where the clocks part at the second order; when bodies move, where here every clock and ruler changes at once (premise 6), while in Einstein's equations a change moves at the speed of light; and at \(L\) and inside it.

At \(L\), general relativity takes a step that nothing outside \(L\) requires. It takes one metric for all observers and continues the same solution, with the same mass, across \(L\) by a change of coordinates. Each frame of reference has its own causal boundary; that step merges the frames' different values into one event horizon, crossed one way only, with no frame at rest inside. Nothing has tested it. The paper names it as the error, and does not take it (Sections 6, 9 and 18).

In its place stand premises 7 and 8. By premise 7, what two frames read is real for each from its own frame of reference. An inferred measurement across \(L\) has two solutions, and they are complex. Premise 8, in the author's words: "Objects can (and do) move beyond their event horizon (also defined as their causal boundary)." \(L\) is the causal boundary, which is also the event horizon, of a frame held at rest: there such a frame would need the speed of light to escape. A frame in free motion needs less. With count \(e\) up to 1, falling in or on its way out, it needs only \(c\,(1-e^2)/(1+e^2)\) along its motion at \(L\), which is \(0.6\,c\) for \(e=0.5\), the count of a body let go from rest at 1,333.3 when \(L=1000\); with \(e\) of 1 or more it needs nothing. The author's picture: "It's like the end of a rainbow. It keeps moving and you never reach it."

A body falling in passes \(L\) on its own clock in a finite time, still moving. Inside \(L\) the pull on its own clock points outward. It slows the body, stops it at \(L/(1+e^2)\), which is 800 for \(e=0.5\) and \(L=1000\), and sends it back out through \(L\) with its count \(e\): the energy for the way out is gained on the journey itself. At the place where it stops, general relativity's continued solution still has it moving inward. The far observer never observes either passage: on its clock it has no finite time (Section 14, Part 7).

A machine that makes energy from nothing

Now the charges. The paper builds a machine that makes energy out of nothing, and it works perfectly, as long as you count wrong (Introduction, thought experiment 2).

(a) Hold two like charges together. They push each other apart, so holding them together stores energy. Call it \(U\).

(b) Lift the pair a height \(h\) above a heavy body, where the pull is \(g\). With \(m\) the mass of the two particles, that costs \(mgh\).

(c) Let them fly apart. The stored energy becomes motion, \(K=U\), and motion with the energy \(K\) has the mass \(K/c^2\). The system just got heavier.

(d) Let them fall back down. The way down gives back \((m+K/c^2)\,gh\).

At the bottom, stop the particles and use their energy of motion to push them together again. You are back at (a), with energy to spare:

\[ \Big(m+\frac{K}{c^2}\Big)gh-m\,gh=\frac{K\,gh}{c^2}\gt0 . \]

Every turn makes that much from nothing. Patent office, please hold.

The way out: the stored energy already had its mass, \(U/c^2\), in step (b). Then the way up costs \((m+U/c^2)\,gh\), the way down gives back the same, and since \(K=U\), nothing is left over. The paper says it in sentences worth writing on the board: "Stored energy has mass before it becomes motion, and when it becomes motion, the motion's mass is exactly the mass the stored energy gives up. That is the cancellation. An account that gives mass to the gain without taking it from the store is this machine."

In the language of step 2: an energy \(E\) held at rest counts far away as \(fE\), so the lift from the clock rate \(f_1\) to \(f_2\) costs \((mc^2+U)(f_2-f_1)\). Leave \(U\) out of the lift, and every turn yields \(U(f_2-f_1)\) from nothing.

Every kind of stored energy, each once

Now put the charges in the jar in place of the heavy body: a cluster of like charges flying apart (Introduction, thought experiment 3). Their energy of motion rises. Nothing crosses the surface, so that gain must be matched by an equal drop in the electrostatic potential energy of the charges' arrangement, counted once, inside the surface. The jar's pull does not change. Treat the energy of motion as newly created mass instead of a transfer from the store, or count the arrangement's energy twice, and the count breaks, and with it the clocks and rulers that the five steps build from it.

It is the falling stone again, with another store. Premise 3, quoted above, names electrostatic potential energy beside gravitational. Through every closed surface around both, two bodies with the counts \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) and a potential energy \(U_{12}\) between them pull as one point mass (close to them, the pull at a given place is not that of one point mass but the pulls of the pieces added, with \(U_{12}\) counted once among them), and the pair's \(L\) is built from all three entries, counted apart (Section 4, statement 5):

\[ L=\frac{2G\,(E_1+E_2+U_{12})}{c^4}. \]

So the count holds every kind of potential energy, each once. The paper's list (Section 2) includes:

  • gravitational potential energy, which in this paper is the clock rate \(f\) of the frame held at rest;
  • electrostatic potential energy, the energy of the charges' arrangement;
  • magnetic interaction energy;
  • nuclear binding energy, already inside a measured nuclear mass, so not added again;
  • the energy a weak decay releases, which stays in the count while the products are inside;
  • scalar potential energy, set by the value of a scalar quantity at each place, the Higgs value included, with the energy of that value's change from place to place and in time counted separately, and a particle's Higgs-related rest energy not counted twice;
  • chemical bond energy;
  • elastic strain energy: a spring stretched by \(x\) holds \(\tfrac12kx^2\), which is \(kx^2/(2c^2)\) of extra mass;
  • surface energy.

"Each once" has teeth. For two bodies held at rest, the potential energy of the pair is in their count once; adding the two rest masses, each at the clock rate the other makes, would hold it twice. Even a body's own gravitational binding counts: a shell of rest mass \(m_0\) held at the radius \(R\) is the point mass \(m_0-Gm_0^2/(2c^2R)\), so an event horizon at the shell needs twice the rest mass one would count without the binding. And for a spinning body the count takes the mass equivalent of all the potential energy that could be converted into kinetic energy.

That is the lesson of the charges. Whatever can become motion was already mass. Leave it out of the count, and you have built the machine.
