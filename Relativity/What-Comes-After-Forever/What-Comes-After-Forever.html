What Comes After Forever?

A photon slips past the edge of A's observable universe. A cannot watch. A can work it out: past the edge, time runs the other way, distances come out imaginary, and every answer comes in two.

An article of the section Relativity of Physics: Particle Reconstruction and General Relativity, written for teachers and curious readers. It comes from the paper Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy.

Forever, on A's clock

Some questions sound as if they belong in a philosophy class. "What comes after forever?" is one of them. This article answers it with arithmetic: the arithmetic of the paper "Reconciling the Schwarzschild Solution with Conservation of Mass-Energy".

Meet A, an observer held at rest deep inside an event horizon. In the thought experiment of the paper's Section 14, the radius of that event horizon is \(L=1{,}000\), in the paper's units, and A sits at 200. Further out are three more places: B at 900, still on A's side; C at 1,000, the edge itself; and D at 1,100, outside. (The Parts named below are the parts of Section 14.)

A switches on a lamp and sends one photon straight outward.

A companion article follows that photon on A's own clock. In the paper's units, where a time is given as the distance light covers in it, it reaches B when A's clock reads 2,758.9, the place 990 at 7,184.1, and 999.99 at 20,979.6. C it reaches at no finite time at all. On A's clock, the photon gets to the edge at forever (Section 14, Part 10). That is a long time to wait for a photon. A, sensibly, does not wait.

C is A's causal boundary, which is also A's event horizon: the edge of A's observable universe. The paper's premise 8 puts it in capital letters: "An observer CANNOT and DOES NOT observe anything passing through the causal boundary."

At C the clock rate of a frame held at rest is zero. That is a zero, not a singularity. No frame can be held at rest exactly there, and nothing in the paper's account stops anything at the edge (Section 14). Objects can (and do) move beyond their event horizon (premise 8), and the photon carries on, through C and out to D. As the paper's author puts it, the photon observes itself passing through A's event horizon; A does not observe it pass.

So what comes after forever? A cannot look. A can, however, do sums, which any physics teacher will tell you is the more useful skill.

Asking the neighbours

The paper's method for anything beyond an edge is to step across it through the frames in between. Section 1 says it in one sentence: "physics beyond a frame's causal boundary can be calculated indirectly by stepping through intermediate frames of reference."

So A asks the neighbours. Each frame reads the photon with its own clock, ruler and scale, and what each one reads is a real number (premise 7). To turn one frame's reading into another's, the paper uses one rule: multiply by the ratio of the two frames' clock rates. The clock rate \(f\), as a share of the clock of an observer at rest far away, obeys \(f^2=1-L/r\), which the conservation laws give (Section 4, statement 6).

On A's side of the edge the sums are ordinary. Bounce the photon off B, and it is home after 5,517.8 on A's own clock (Part 10). B reads A's photon at six times A's energy, \(6E_0\) (Part 2). The frame at 990 reads it at \(19.900\,E_0\), the frame at 999.99 at \(632.452\,E_0\). Nothing is added from outside: at each step the photon carries on the energy that the frame there reads (Part 3). In the author's words: "Our math says that nothing can escape the black hole without using energy obtained during the journey out."

Across the edge, the sums change character. Take A and D:

\[ \frac{f_A^{\,2}}{f_D^{\,2}}=\frac{1-1000/200}{1-1000/1100}=\frac{-4}{1/11}=-44 . \]

The square of the conversion factor is negative. No ordinary number does that: \(2\times2=4\), and \((-2)\times(-2)=4\) as well. It takes the number \(i\), whose square is \(-1\). There are two of those, \(+i\) and \(-i\), because \((-i)\times(-i)=-1\) too. So the factor is \(\pm\sqrt{44}\,i\), and what A works out for D's reading of the photon is

\[ E_D=\pm\sqrt{44}\,i\,E_0=\pm6.633\,i\,E_0 . \]

Two answers, both imaginary (Part 3). D itself, reading the photon with its own instruments, gets one real number (premise 7). The \(i\) lives in A's working-out, made across A's edge.

The neighbours do not change the answer. Move the two frames that straddle C nearer to it or farther from it, and the numbers for them swing wildly. A's answer for D does not budge:

Each neighbour's clock rate enters the chain once above the line and once below it, and cancels (Part 3). A more energetic photon changes nothing here either: double \(E_0\), and every reading on every leg doubles, D's included, while C, where the factors vanish or grow without bound, stays where it is (Part 4). Premise 7, in the paper's version for a child, sums it up: when you work out something about a place beyond your edge, using someone else's measurements on the way, "you always get two answers."

Time runs the other way

Now the clocks. Follow the photon on A's side of the edge with two clocks at once: A's own, and the clock of the far observer, at rest far away out past D, on the other side of A's edge. As the photon moves outward, A's clock runs forward. The far clock, worked out along the same path, runs backward:

\[ dT_A=-\,|f_A|\,dt=-2\,dt . \]

Here \(T_A\) is A's time, \(t\) the far observer's, and \(|f_A|=2\) the real size of A's clock rate, whose square is \(-4\). For every tick forward on A's clock, the far clock goes half a tick back. The paper's verdict: "time flips across the causal boundary" (Part 10).

The same flip shows in the light's speed as the far observer works it out. On the far observer's ruler and clock, light along the radius moves at \(c\,(1-L/r)\) (Part 10). For light moving outward, that speed is positive outside the edge and negative inside:

In the author's words, "time reverses". The math carries it.

Distances turn imaginary

Rulers next. A frame held at rest reads its own ruler along the radius at the real size of its clock rate, \(d\ell=dr/|f|\), a rule the paper adopts with its premises (Section 3, premise 7). The paper writes this one out from the far side of the edge, looking in at A's side:

\[ d\ell=\frac{dr}{\sqrt{L/r-1}}\ \ \text{(read on A's side)},\qquad \frac{dr}{f}=\mp\,i\,\frac{dr}{\sqrt{L/r-1}}\ \ \text{(worked out from the far side)}. \]

The frames on A's side read a real length. Worked out from across the edge, the very same step is that length times \(\mp i\) (Part 10). In the author's words, "the distance becomes imaginary". The math carries this too.

The clocks go the same way: what the far observer works out for a stretch of A's clock is exactly \(\mp i\) times A's own reading (Part 10).

A picture for the classroom. Lay the ordinary numbers along a line, left to right. The imaginary numbers sit on a second line, at right angles to the first. Across the edge, the clock steps and the ruler steps both swing onto that second line. The paper's phrase: past its causal boundary, the value "moves into the complex dimension" (Part 10).

The edge is one edge, seen from two sides: "C is A's causal boundary, as \(L\) is the far observer's" (Part 10). A, looking out past C, stands under the same premise as the far observer looking in. Any inferred measurement made about an event beyond the causal boundary, through an intermediate frame, "always will have two solutions and involve complex numbers" (premise 7). The energy A works out for D, \(\pm6.633\,i\,E_0\), is one such measurement. Every distance and every time that A works out past C is another. In the paper's version for a teacher, converting a reading across a causal boundary "multiplies it by a factor whose square is negative. So the result has two solutions, \(+i\) and \(-i\) times a real number" (premise 7). So, for A too, a distance worked out past C comes out imaginary.

Not everything swings. Each frame's own readings stay real and single (premise 7). The light's speed, which depends only on \(f^2\), is the same real number in both solutions (Part 10).

Why two answers?

Every answer worked out across the edge has come in a pair. The paper explains why in three steps (Part 10).

First, the clock rate. The conservation laws fix its square, \(f^2=1-L/r\). Past the edge the square is negative, and a negative number has two square roots, \(+i\sqrt{L/r-1}\) and \(-i\sqrt{L/r-1}\). The premises refuse to pick a favourite; in the paper's words, they "prefer neither".

Second, time flips, as above.

Third, the complex dimension. On the far clock, the light's time grows without bound toward the edge from both sides, and the edge does not say how the two sides join. Every way around it adds an odd multiple of \(i\pi L/c\) to the far time, never a real amount. The paper joins the two sides with one rule, adopted with its premises: the far time and the clock rate go past the edge together, along one half-turn, above or below, with no further winding. One half-turn adds \(+i\pi L/c\), the other \(-i\pi L/c\), and each goes with one root of the clock rate. In the version for a child, the far clock's time "cannot go straight through the edge, so it has to go around it, one way or the other, and each way gives one of the two forms."

You can see the half-turn in the time of the photon's trip from A to D, on the far clock:

\[ c\,\Delta t=900-1000\ln 8\mp1000\pi\,i=-1{,}179.4\mp3{,}141.6\,i . \]

The \(\mp1000\pi\,i\) is the half-turn: \(\pi L\), with \(L=1{,}000\). Scale this time by A's clock rate, as the far observer works that rate out, \(f_A=\pm2i\), and it becomes \(6{,}283.2\mp2{,}358.9\,i\): two solutions, with the same real part, \(2000\pi\), on both (Part 10). Every value worked out for the photon across the edge comes in two forms like these, complex conjugates of each other, one for each half-turn. Each frame's own readings stay real and single.

The author pictures the pair this way: "there are two solutions, which makes sense because the photon exists in two places at every point in time", "because it started going back in time after it passed thru the event horizon at t=infinity."

The reason he gives is in the paper's math, both halves of it. On A's clock the photon reaches the edge at no finite time: \(T_A=\infty\) (Part 10). Past the edge, time flips (Part 10), and that flip is the second of the paper's three steps to the two solutions.

And momentum?

Conservation of momentum is one of the two laws the whole paper starts from: the total momentum of a closed system does not change (Section 2). The author's picture of how the photon's momentum is kept across the edge, in his words: "This preserves conservation of momentum because the magnitude of the distance decreases and time goes in reverse." Earlier, on the same calculation: "What we find is that momentum is conserved because it starts moving in an imaginary direction." And: "some attributes turn imaginary, and the distance time or math turns complex."

The math says which attribute does what, worked out across the edge:

  • Time runs the other way: \(dT_A=-2\,dt\) along the photon (Part 10).
  • The distance turns imaginary: a ruler step is \(\mp i\) times the real one (Part 10), a step along the imaginary line of the classroom picture.
  • The clock steps turn imaginary: \(\mp i\) times A's own reading (Part 10).
  • The energy A works out for D turns imaginary: \(\pm6.633\,i\,E_0\) (Part 3).
  • The far time of the trip turns complex: a real part, plus the half-turn's \(\mp1000\pi\,i\) (Part 10).
  • What stays real: the light's speed, the same number in both solutions (Part 10), and every frame's own readings (premise 7).

Time going in reverse, and motion in an imaginary direction: both are in the math. In the author's picture, together they are what keep the photon's momentum.

What general relativity says instead

General relativity tells this story differently, and the paper puts the difference in one step. The Schwarzschild form is derived outside the event horizon. General relativity then carries it inside by a change of coordinates (Section 7, parts 15 to 17). With that step every frame gets one event horizon, crossed one way only; no frame is left at rest inside; and, with the direction of time it chooses there, everything is sent to the centre (Section 18).

In that account there is no A, because nothing can be held at rest inside. There is no photon reaching D, because the horizon is one-way. And one account is carried across the edge, where the paper has premise 7's two complex solutions.

The paper keeps A, with its own real clock, ruler and scale, and puts premises 7 and 8 where that step was. The burden sits on the step, not on the paper: none of it is needed outside the horizon, where the solution was already right (Section 18).

What could decide between them? Anything, light or matter, arriving outside from inside an event horizon, or any signal from a frame held at rest inside one (Section 18). In the thought experiment, A's photon reaching D is that event.
